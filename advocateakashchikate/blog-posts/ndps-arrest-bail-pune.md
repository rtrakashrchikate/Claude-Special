# NDPS Act Arrest in Pune — What Are Your Rights and Can You Get Bail?

If you or someone you care about has been arrested under the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985 — commonly called the NDPS Act — the first thing you need to know is this: NDPS is not ordinary criminal law. Bail is harder to obtain, the burden of proof shifts in unusual ways, and the consequences of a misstep in the early hours after arrest can affect your case for months. This guide explains what the law actually says, what your rights are, and where courts in Pune draw the line between an accused who can get bail and one who cannot.

---

## Why NDPS Is Different From Other Criminal Cases

Most criminal offences in India follow the rule that an accused is presumed innocent until proven guilty, and bail is available unless the prosecution can show specific reasons to deny it. The NDPS Act partially reverses this logic.

Under Section 37 of the NDPS Act, for any offence punishable with imprisonment of ten years or more — which covers commercial quantity cases and several other serious charges — the court cannot grant bail unless it is satisfied of two things simultaneously:

1. There are **reasonable grounds to believe that the accused is not guilty** of such offence.
2. The accused **is not likely to commit any offence** while on bail.

Both conditions must be met. Neither one alone is sufficient. This is sometimes called the "twin test" or "dual negative conditions" of Section 37, and it is the single biggest legal obstacle you face in an NDPS arrest.

This is the opposite of the usual bail framework. In a normal criminal case, the prosecution must convince the court to *deny* bail. In a serious NDPS case, you must convince the court to *grant* it by meeting these two conditions affirmatively.

## Small Quantity vs. Commercial Quantity — Why It Matters Enormously

The NDPS Act categorises drug seizures by weight, and that weight determines everything: the maximum sentence you face, which court has bail jurisdiction, and whether Section 37 applies at all.

- **Small quantity:** The maximum punishment is one year in prison. Section 37 does *not* apply. Bail is available on ordinary terms.
- **Intermediate quantity** (more than small, less than commercial): The maximum punishment is up to ten years. Section 37 *may* apply depending on the specific charges.
- **Commercial quantity:** The minimum mandatory punishment is ten years, and it goes up to twenty years. Section 37 *fully* applies. Bail is the hardest to obtain.

For heroin, a small quantity is 5 grams; commercial quantity is 250 grams. For ganja (cannabis), the thresholds are 1 kilogram and 20 kilograms respectively. Other substances have their own limits defined in a central government notification. The first thing any lawyer defending an NDPS accused must confirm is the exact quantity seized and what category it falls into — because the entire bail strategy depends on it.

## Your Role in the Case: Carrier, Consumer, or Supplier?

Courts do not treat all NDDS accused the same, even in commercial quantity cases. The alleged role of the accused in the drug transaction is one of the strongest arguments available in bail hearings. Judges at both the Pune Sessions Court and the Bombay High Court regularly distinguish between:

- **The carrier or courier:** A person transporting drugs without knowledge of the full chain, often a first-time accused with no prior record, who was intercepted at a checkpoint or on a highway.
- **The consumer:** A person found in possession of a small or intermediate quantity intended for personal use, with no evidence of sale or supply.
- **The supplier or dealer:** A person with evidence of habitual supply, financial links to a drug network, multiple seizures, or communication records showing organised distribution.

The law does not formally create these categories, but courts use them every day in practice. A carrier who demonstrates no knowledge of the nature of the substance, or a consumer who had no intention to sell, stands in a materially different position from someone who is the target of a Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB) or State ATS investigation into a drug network.

## Common NDPS Cases in Pune

Pune sees a wide range of NDPS matters. Road check seizures — particularly on the Mumbai-Pune Expressway, Satara Road, and Solapur Road — are among the most frequent. Hotel and residential premises raids, often based on informer tip-offs, make up another large category. Cases involving Pune airport and courier shipments have grown, as have cases at the borders of Maharashtra. Each setting creates different evidentiary questions: Was the search legal? Was the seizure witnessed? Was the panchanama conducted properly? These procedural points often become critical in bail hearings.

## What to Do Immediately After an NDPS Arrest

The hours immediately after arrest are the most consequential. Follow these steps without exception:

**Do not give any statement to the police or NCB without a lawyer present.** Statements made to police officers are not admissible as confessions under the NDPS Act — but they can be used to guide further investigation, link you to others, or construct a narrative that is hard to undo. Stay silent.

**Contact a criminal defence lawyer at the earliest.** Under the law, you have the right to be informed of the grounds of arrest and to consult a legal practitioner of your choice. This right exists from the moment of arrest.

**Understand what was seized and under which section you have been charged.** Ask the lawyer to obtain the seizure memo, the panchanama, and the First Information Report or remand application as soon as possible. The quantity seized, the substance identified, and the sections invoked will determine the entire course of your defence and bail application.

## Where to Apply for Bail in NDPS Cases

A Judicial Magistrate has limited bail powers in NDPS matters. For offences punishable with ten years or more, the Sessions Court is the first forum with jurisdiction to hear a bail application. In Pune, this is the Sessions Court at Shivajinagar.

If the Sessions Court rejects bail — which happens frequently in commercial quantity cases — the next step is the Bombay High Court. The High Court hears NDPS bail applications at its Nagpur bench for some matters, but most Pune cases go before the principal bench in Mumbai.

In certain matters involving national-level agencies like the NCB, or where the offence is alleged to fall under special provisions, the High Court may be the appropriate first forum.

## When the Bombay High Court Grants Bail in NDPS Cases

Despite the strict conditions in Section 37, the Bombay High Court does grant bail in NDPS cases. The patterns that emerge from reported decisions are:

- The accused is a **first-time offender** with no prior criminal record and the quantity, though commercial, is close to the lower threshold.
- The **investigation is complete** and the chargesheet has been filed, removing any risk of the accused influencing witnesses or evidence.
- There is **no evidence of a larger conspiracy** or network — the accused appears to have had a limited, possibly peripheral role.
- The accused has **deep roots in the community** — a stable family, a known address, employment, and no realistic flight risk.
- **Medical conditions** that cannot be adequately treated in custody.
- Lengthy **pre-trial incarceration** disproportionate to the likely sentence even upon conviction.

No single factor guarantees bail. Experienced NDPS defence lawyers build applications that combine several of these elements while directly addressing the Section 37 twin test — arguing affirmatively that there are grounds to believe the accused may not be guilty, rather than simply arguing the person is not dangerous.

## Default Bail — A Right You Cannot Afford to Miss

This is one of the most important and most missed rights in NDPS cases. Under **Section 187(3) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)** — which corresponds to **Section 167(2) of the old Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC)** — if the police or prosecution fail to file a chargesheet within the prescribed time limit, the accused becomes entitled to bail as a matter of right.

The time limits are:
- **60 days** from the date of arrest for offences punishable with less than ten years.
- **90 days** from the date of arrest for offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or ten years or more.

This is called **default bail** or **indefeasible bail**, and it cannot be denied if the chargesheet has genuinely not been filed. The accused must apply for it before the chargesheet is filed — once the prosecution files the chargesheet, even if it is a day late, courts have held that the right is lost.

In practice, agencies investigating NDPS cases are aware of these deadlines and usually file a chargesheet or at least a supplementary chargesheet within time. But not always. Your lawyer must track these dates from Day 1.

## Practical Timeline: Arrest to Bail

- **Day 1:** Arrest, seizure, panchanama. You will be produced before a Magistrate, usually within 24 hours. Police or NCB will seek remand (custody). The Magistrate will grant remand — typically 14 days or less for police custody, with extensions possible.
- **Days 2–14:** Interrogation during remand period. Lawyer engages with the case, collects documents, files bail application before the Sessions Court.
- **Week 2–3:** Sessions Court bail hearing. Arguments on Section 37, quantity, role, criminal record. Judgement typically within days of hearing.
- **If rejected:** Bail application filed at Bombay High Court within days of Sessions rejection. Mention before the court for an early date. Interim bail pending final disposal is often the immediate goal.
- **HC hearing:** Within 2–4 weeks of filing, depending on urgency and docket.

The total time from arrest to an interim HC order, if the Sessions Court rejects bail, is typically four to eight weeks. This is not a guarantee, but it is a realistic working estimate for a Pune NDPS matter.

## FAQ

### Q: Can my family member get bail in a commercial quantity NDPS case?

Bail in a commercial quantity NDPS case is difficult but not impossible. The Section 37 twin test must be satisfied, but courts do grant bail where the accused is a first-time offender with no prior record, the investigation is complete, and there is no evidence of a large distribution network. The strength of the bail application — both the legal arguments and the supporting material — is decisive.

### Q: The police said a statement was recorded. Can that be used against my family member?

Statements made to police officers are generally not admissible as confessions in court. Under Section 25 of the Indian Evidence Act (and the corresponding provision under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam), a confession to a police officer cannot be proved against an accused. However, statements made to officers of certain agencies, including customs officers or Department of Revenue Intelligence (DRI) officers in specific circumstances, may be treated differently. This distinction must be examined by a lawyer on the facts of each case.

### Q: What is the difference between police custody remand and judicial custody?

When police or NCB seek remand, they first ask for "police custody" — meaning the accused stays in their custody for interrogation. A Magistrate can grant up to 15 days of police custody in NDPS matters. After that, the accused is sent to "judicial custody," meaning they are held in prison (Yerawada, in the case of most Pune arrests) while the investigation continues. Bail applications are typically filed during judicial custody.

### Q: What if the seizure was made from a vehicle shared by multiple people?

This is a common situation in road check cases. When drugs are found in a shared vehicle, the prosecution must establish that each accused had knowledge of the drugs and had some degree of possession or control. Courts distinguish between an accused who was merely present and one who was an active participant. This is a factual and legal argument that can be made both in bail hearings and at trial. It does not automatically result in bail, but it is a meaningful defence that courts take seriously.
