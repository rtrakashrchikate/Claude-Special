# Divorce Process in Pune: Timeline, Courts & What to Expect (2026)

Deciding to end a marriage is one of the most difficult decisions a person can make. What makes it harder is not knowing what the process actually looks like — how long it takes, what the courts will ask of you, whether you can protect your children and your finances, and whether your spouse's refusal to cooperate changes everything.

This guide gives you realistic, honest answers before you take the first step. It is written for people in Pune who are at the beginning of this process and want to understand what they are walking into — not to be told what they want to hear.

---

## Types of Divorce in India

Before anything else, the law that applies to your divorce depends on how your marriage was solemnised and what religion you and your spouse follow.

**Mutual Consent Divorce under Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955** applies to Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs, and Jains. This is the fastest route — both parties agree to separate, agree on terms, and file a joint petition. No grounds need to be established. It is, as the name says, entirely consensual.

**Contested Divorce under Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act** applies to the same community but in circumstances where one party does not consent, or where the marriage has broken down and the filing party needs to establish a legal ground before the court will grant a decree.

**Divorce under the Special Marriage Act, 1954** governs civil marriages and inter-religion marriages. The process is broadly similar to HMA divorce, and the Pune Family Court at Shivajinagar handles these matters in the same complex.

A note on recent legal changes: the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita came into force in 2024 and replaced the Indian Penal Code for criminal matters. Divorce law itself falls under the Hindu Marriage Act and related personal law statutes, which have not changed. BNS changes are not relevant to your divorce proceedings.

## Grounds for Divorce Under Indian Law

In mutual consent divorce, grounds are irrelevant — you and your spouse agree to part, and that is sufficient. In contested proceedings, however, the court will not grant a decree unless you can prove one of the recognised grounds. The choice of ground is therefore a strategic decision, not just a factual one.

**Cruelty** is the most commonly invoked ground in contested divorce petitions across Pune and Maharashtra. Cruelty includes physical violence, but courts have consistently held that persistent mental harassment, verbal abuse, humiliation in front of relatives, financial control amounting to deprivation, and false allegations against the petitioner or their family also constitute cruelty. A single incident of extreme violence can suffice; so can a sustained pattern of conduct over years.

**Desertion** requires proof that the respondent has wilfully deserted the petitioner continuously for at least two years immediately before the petition is filed. It must be deliberate abandonment — not a separation agreed to by both parties, and not a departure caused by the petitioner's own conduct.

**Adultery** requires proving voluntary sexual intercourse with a third person. Since direct proof is rarely available, courts have developed well-settled case law on what circumstantial evidence suffices. This ground is available but rarely pursued in Pune Family Court today — cruelty or irretrievable breakdown is typically more effective.

**Mental disorder** requires medical evidence that the respondent suffers from a condition of a nature and degree that makes continued cohabitation unreasonably impossible. Courts require proper psychiatric evidence and will not accept lay descriptions alone.

**Irretrievable breakdown of marriage** is worth noting separately. While it has not been formally enacted as a statutory ground in the HMA, the Supreme Court has in a series of decisions exercised its Article 142 powers to grant divorce on this basis where marriages are clearly and completely dead. This is increasingly available as a practical remedy, particularly in cases before the High Court.

## Mutual Consent Divorce — Step by Step

This is the path most couples who have already agreed to separate choose to take, and it is genuinely the most efficient process when handled properly.

**Step 1 — Joint petition filed at Pune Family Court.** The Pune Family Court is located at the District Court complex in Shivajinagar. Both parties must sign the petition. It sets out the date of marriage, the fact of separation, and the agreed terms — maintenance if any, division of assets, and child custody arrangements. Getting these terms right at this stage prevents disputes later.

**Step 2 — First motion hearing.** Both parties appear before the Family Court judge in person. The judge records their statements confirming that the petition reflects their genuine, free consent. This appearance is not optional — both parties must be physically present, or must apply in advance for permission to appear by video conference.

**Step 3 — The six-month cooling-off period.** After the first motion, Section 13B(2) HMA mandates a six-month waiting period before the parties can move for the second motion. The intent is to allow reconciliation. In practice, for couples who have been separated for years and have already divided their lives, this feels like an unnecessary delay.

The cooling-off period can be waived. The Supreme Court in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur (2017) held that courts have the power to waive this period where reconciliation is clearly not possible. Waiver applications are now routinely made before the High Court, and increasingly before the Family Court itself in appropriate cases.

**Step 4 — Second motion and decree.** After the cooling-off period (or its waiver), both parties appear again and confirm their consent remains. The court then passes the decree of divorce.

**Timeline:** With the cooling-off period, mutual consent divorce typically takes 7 to 18 months from petition to decree. If cooling-off is successfully waived, the process can be completed in 3 to 6 months. If either party withdraws consent before the second motion, the joint petition collapses and the matter must restart as a contested case.

## Contested Divorce — What to Expect

If your spouse refuses to consent, or if the circumstances of the marriage make consent-based proceedings inappropriate, contested divorce is the route. Go in with realistic expectations about the timeline.

**Step 1 — Petition and service.** The petitioning party files a divorce petition at the Pune Family Court. The court issues notice to the respondent. Proper service on the respondent is a step that frequently causes delay — if the respondent evades service, proceedings can be held up substantially.

**Step 2 — Written statement.** Once served, the respondent files their written statement responding to the petition's allegations. They may also file a counter-petition raising grounds of their own.

**Step 3 — Mandatory mediation.** The Family Court Act requires parties in matrimonial disputes to undergo mediation before trial. The Pune Mediation Centre at the District Court complex handles these referrals. Mediation is more successful than most people expect — a significant number of contested matters settle here or convert to mutual consent petitions.

**Step 4 — Evidence stage.** If mediation does not resolve matters, both parties file their evidence — affidavits-in-chief, documents, and witnesses. Cross-examination follows. This is usually the longest stage of the proceeding.

**Step 5 — Arguments and judgment.** After evidence, both sides file written arguments and make oral submissions. The court then reserves and delivers its judgment.

**Timeline:** A full contested divorce trial in Pune can take 3 to 7 years. This is a realistic estimate, not a worst-case figure. Interim relief — maintenance, custody, residence orders — is available from the early stages and should be applied for promptly; do not wait for the final decree.

## What Happens During Divorce — Interim Orders

You do not need to wait for the divorce decree to secure financial protection or custody arrangements. Courts can grant interim orders at early hearings, and in many cases these orders matter more than the final decree.

**Interim maintenance under Section 24 HMA** can be applied for at the first hearing. A spouse without adequate independent income — whether the wife or the husband — is entitled to maintenance pendente lite during the proceedings. Courts determine this quickly, and orders are typically made effective from the date of application. In Pune Family Court, interim maintenance orders are frequently obtained within one to three hearings.

**Interim custody** is determined with the child's welfare as the paramount consideration. Courts at the early stage often direct that both parents have access, that the child remain in a specified location, or that one parent have primary residence pending trial. Neither parent should remove the child from Pune or from India without the court's express permission once proceedings are underway.

**Residence protection under the Domestic Violence Act.** Section 19 of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 allows a wife to seek a residence order preventing her eviction from the matrimonial home — even if she does not own it, and even before the divorce decree is passed. This protection is available independently of the divorce proceedings and can be obtained urgently.

**Injunctions against disposal of property.** If there is a risk that your spouse will sell, transfer, or encumber jointly held property during the proceedings, the court can grant an injunction preventing this. Such applications should be made early and supported by evidence of the specific risk.

## Evidence in Divorce Cases — What Courts Look For

People often ask what evidence they should be gathering. The answer depends on the ground being argued.

For cruelty cases, courts look at documentary evidence of abuse — medical reports, police complaints (even if not pursued), text messages and WhatsApp chats showing abusive language, witness statements from family members or neighbours who observed incidents, and any formal complaints made to local bodies or women's help lines. In the absence of physical violence, the cumulative pattern of conduct matters and must be built up through consistent evidence.

For financial claims — maintenance, property rights — courts need income evidence for both parties. Salary slips, ITR filings, bank statements, property registration documents, and investment records are all relevant. If you believe your spouse is concealing income or assets, a disclosure application can be filed before the court.

For custody disputes, courts look at the living arrangements available to each parent, the child's school and social environment, the parenting capacity of each party, and — for children above a certain age — the child's own expressed preferences.

## NRI Divorce — Important Differences

If one or both spouses live outside India, the divorce process has additional complications.

A foreign divorce decree — obtained in the United States, United Kingdom, or Canada, for example — is not automatically valid in India for a marriage solemnised under Indian law. Indian courts apply the doctrine of lack of jurisdiction in many such cases, particularly where the Indian-based spouse did not genuinely submit to the foreign court's jurisdiction or did not receive adequate notice of the foreign proceedings.

If your marriage was registered in India under the Hindu Marriage Act or Special Marriage Act, you will generally need Indian court proceedings to obtain a divorce that is fully valid under Indian law — regardless of what any foreign court has said.

Service of court summons on a spouse living abroad requires specific procedures — service through diplomatic channels, by email where courts permit, or through the Indian embassy. This causes delays but can be managed with proper legal representation. A Pune Family Court proceeding can advance even when the foreign-resident spouse refuses to engage, including the possibility of a decree being passed ex parte after proper service is established.

## Maintenance After Divorce — Permanent Alimony and What to Expect

Interim maintenance (during proceedings) and permanent alimony (post-decree) are two separate entitlements. Many people going through divorce in Pune focus on the divorce itself and do not plan adequately for what comes after. This section addresses post-divorce financial arrangements.

**Permanent alimony under Section 25 HMA** can be claimed by either spouse — husband or wife — after the decree of divorce. The court can grant a lump sum or periodic payment. The factors the court considers include the income and property of both parties, the conduct of the parties during the marriage, the duration of the marriage, the standard of living maintained, and the health and earning capacity of the claimant.

There is no formula. Courts in Pune have awarded anything from nominal amounts to significant monthly maintenance or substantial lump sums, depending entirely on the financial circumstances of the parties. A spouse who gave up a career to manage the household and raise children is in a stronger position than the same spouse who maintained their own career and income throughout.

Maintenance can be modified if circumstances change significantly — if the recipient remarries, the obligation ends. If the payer's income changes substantially, either party can apply for revision.

**Maintenance for children** is separate from spousal maintenance and continues until the child reaches adulthood (and in some cases beyond, for daughters not yet married, or children with special needs). Both parents share the financial responsibility for their children regardless of who has custody.

**Enforcement of maintenance orders.** If maintenance is ordered and not paid, the defaulting spouse can be proceeded against for execution. Courts can attach property, direct payment from salary, and in persistent cases, commit the defaulter to civil prison. Enforcement of maintenance orders in Pune is a practical area that requires active legal follow-up — court orders alone do not compel compliance.

One practical point often overlooked: maintenance negotiations during mutual consent divorce are binding only if properly recorded in the petition and in the decree. Informal agreements made outside the court process are very difficult to enforce later. Whatever is agreed should be captured formally.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can I get divorced even if my spouse refuses to sign papers?**
A: Yes. If your spouse refuses mutual consent, you can file a contested petition under Section 13 HMA establishing one of the recognised grounds. Your spouse's refusal to participate does not stop the case — after proper service of notice, the proceedings continue even in the respondent's absence. A divorce decree can be granted on the basis of ex parte evidence if the respondent consistently fails to appear.

**Q: What happens to joint property — flat, car, savings — during divorce?**
A: India does not have a community property regime — assets are not automatically divided 50-50. Courts consider who purchased the asset, whose money funded it, and whether there was a shared contribution. In mutual consent divorce, the parties agree on division themselves and record it in the petition. In contested proceedings, a separate application for partition or injunction may be needed. Seeking legal advice about your specific property situation at the outset is strongly recommended.

**Q: Who gets the children while the divorce is going on?**
A: The law treats custody as a separate question from divorce, determined on the basis of the child's welfare above all else. Courts typically make interim custody arrangements at early hearings. In Pune Family Court's experience, young children generally remain with the mother pending trial, but this is not a fixed rule — the specific facts, the child's age, each parent's working situation, and the child's own preferences (for older children) all factor in.

**Q: How much does divorce cost in Pune — lawyer fees plus court fees?**
A: Court filing fees at the Pune Family Court are modest — typically a few hundred to a few thousand rupees depending on the reliefs sought. Lawyer fees vary significantly depending on whether the matter is mutual consent or contested, the complexity of financial and custody issues, and how long proceedings take. Mutual consent divorce is substantially less expensive than full contested proceedings. We provide transparent fee estimates at the first consultation before any commitment is made.

**Q: Can the six-month cooling-off period in mutual consent divorce be waived?**
A: Yes. Following Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur (2017), courts have discretion to waive the cooling-off period where reconciliation is not possible. We routinely apply for waiver in appropriate cases — where parties have been separated for a long period, have already divided assets and settled custody, and where there is no minor child whose interests require the additional time. The High Court can grant this waiver even where the Family Court has declined to do so.
