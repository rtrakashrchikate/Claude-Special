# Cheque Bounce Case in Pune (NI Act S.138): Complete Guide for 2026

Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act is the single most common criminal case in Pune's Magistrate courts. Walk into any JMFC court in Shivajinagar on a Monday morning and a third of the matters on the board will be cheque dishonour cases — involving everyone from individual borrowers to large real estate developers. If you have received a legal notice for cheque bounce, or if someone owes you money on a dishonoured cheque, this guide covers both sides of the dispute completely.

**What this guide covers:**

- How a dishonoured cheque legally becomes a criminal case (and what can defeat it)
- What to do if you are the complainant trying to recover money
- Defences available to the accused that actually work in Pune courts
- Bail rights and what happens if you ignore summons
- Compounding — the most practical resolution, and why timing matters
- Realistic timelines at Pune's JMFC courts

> **2026 Update:** The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) has renamed certain procedural sections, but **Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act itself is unchanged**. Everything in this guide is current law.

## How a Cheque Bounce Becomes a Criminal Case — The 4 Triggers

Not every dishonoured cheque automatically becomes a criminal case. The law requires four specific conditions to be met in strict sequence. If any condition is missing, the complaint is liable to be dismissed — even after years of litigation.

### Trigger 1 — The Bank Returns the Cheque with a Dishonour Memo

The bank must return the cheque with a written dishonour memo citing one of the legally recognised grounds:

- "Insufficient funds"
- "Account closed"
- "Payment stopped by drawer"
- "Signature mismatch"

If the cheque is returned for a purely technical reason unrelated to funds — such as a date error — Section 138 may not be attracted.

### Trigger 2 — Statutory Demand Notice Within 30 Days

The payee must send a **written demand notice** to the drawer within **30 days** of receiving the dishonour memo. This notice must be sent by registered post (RPAD). It must demand payment of the cheque amount.

### Trigger 3 — Drawer Fails to Pay Within 15 Days

The drawer (cheque issuer) must fail to pay the full amount within **15 days** of receiving the demand notice. This "grace period" is the last chance to avoid criminal prosecution. Payment in full during this window ends the matter.

### Trigger 4 — Complaint Filed Within 30 Days of Grace Period Expiry

The payee must file the criminal complaint in the competent Magistrate's Court within **30 days** of the 15-day grace period expiring. Miss this window and the complaint is time-barred.

> **All four conditions are mandatory and sequential.** A complaint that skips or bundles any one of them is vulnerable to dismissal at any stage — before trial, at the charge stage, or even on appeal.

## If You Are the Complainant — How to Win

The most common reason complainants lose S.138 cases in Pune is a procedural defect, not a lack of merit. Courts are strict. Here is how to give yourself the strongest possible position.

**Send the demand notice correctly.** Use **RPAD — registered post with acknowledgment due** — and keep both the dispatch receipt and the returned acknowledgment card. If the card is not returned (the accused refused or did not collect), keep the envelope and the postal tracking record. Courts treat the date of tendering at the post office as the date of service where the letter is refused or not collected.

**Use supplementary channels.** In addition to RPAD, send the notice simultaneously by email and WhatsApp and take timestamped screenshots of delivery and read receipts. Pune courts — and the Bombay High Court — have increasingly accepted electronic evidence as corroboration, especially where the accused claims non-receipt.

**Preserve original documents — never copies.** Keep the **original cheque** and the **original bank dishonour memo**. Courts require originals to be produced in evidence. A photocopy is not sufficient and will create serious evidentiary problems at trial.

**Strictly observe the 30-day filing window.** Map out your dates carefully:

1. Date of dishonour memo → +30 days → latest date to send notice
2. Date of sending notice → +15 days → end of grace period
3. End of grace period → +30 days → last date to file complaint in court

**If the accused evades service,** discuss substitute service options with your lawyer — courts can permit service through newspaper publication or a known relative in appropriate cases.

## If You Are the Accused — Defences That Work

Being named as accused in a cheque bounce case is not the end. Several defences are available, and the right one depends entirely on your specific facts.

**No legally enforceable debt or liability.** If the cheque was issued as a security deposit (for a tenancy, for example), as a blank signed cheque later misused, or in a context where no lawful debt existed, the complainant cannot succeed. This is the most powerful defence but requires you to rebut the legal presumption under Section 139 NI Act — the court presumes the cheque was for a debt unless you prove otherwise.

**Improper service of the demand notice.** If the notice was sent to a wrong or outdated address, or if you genuinely did not receive it for reasons attributable to the complainant (such as sending it to an old address after being informed of your new address), the complaint can fail at a preliminary stage. Proper service is a condition precedent to the offence crystallising.

**Limitation — complaint filed too late.** If the complaint was filed even one day after the 30-day window expired, it is time-barred. Compute the dates precisely from the documents on record.

**Director's liability — company cheques.** Under **Section 141 NI Act (vicarious liability)**, only those directors who were "in charge of and responsible for the conduct of the business" at the relevant time can be prosecuted for a company's dishonoured cheque. Being a non-executive, nominee, or paper director with no day-to-day financial control is a substantive defence — but it must be pleaded specifically and supported by documents (board resolutions, appointment terms, resignation letters).

**Compounding.** In most practical cases, the best outcome for both sides is settlement. The accused pays the cheque amount (sometimes with interest or additional compensation), both parties file a joint application, and the case is closed without a conviction.

## Bail in NI Act S.138 Cases

Section 138 is a **bailable offence** — one of the most frequently misunderstood points among accused persons. You cannot be held in custody without the possibility of bail simply because a cheque bounce complaint has been filed.

In practice, Pune Magistrate courts first issue a **summons** (not an arrest warrant) requiring the accused to appear on a specified date. Many accused persons ignore the summons — either not taking it seriously or hoping the matter will go away. This is a serious mistake. If you do not appear on the summons date:

1. The court upgrades to a **bailable warrant**
2. If that is also ignored, to a **non-bailable warrant**
3. At the NBW stage, arrest becomes inevitable

If you appear on the summons date, bail is applied for and almost always granted — either on a personal bond with a surety, or sometimes on depositing a portion of the cheque amount into court as security. Your lawyer can negotiate these terms at the first hearing.

> **Do not ignore court summons.** Non-appearance turns a manageable civil-style dispute into a warrant situation that is far harder and costlier to resolve.

## Compounding — The Standard Resolution

Section 147 of the Negotiable Instruments Act expressly makes S.138 offences **compoundable** — the parties can settle at any stage of the proceedings with the court's permission.

**How compounding works:**

1. Accused and complainant negotiate a settlement amount (usually the cheque value plus interest or compensation)
2. Both parties file a joint application before the court
3. Court records the settlement and dismisses the case
4. **No conviction is recorded**

The court fee for the compounding application is nominal. The entire process typically takes one to two hearings.

**Why timing matters enormously:** A **conviction under Section 138** is a criminal record. It appears in police verification reports, affects eligibility for government tenders and contracts, and is cited in future loan and business due diligence. The sooner the matter is compounded, the lower the compensation the complainant will typically demand — and the lower the legal costs for both sides. The ideal compounding window in Pune courts is before charge is framed.

## How Long Does a Cheque Bounce Case Take in Pune?

Cheque bounce cases are handled by **Judicial Magistrate First Class (JMFC) courts** at the Shivajinagar complex in Pune. These courts carry extremely heavy dockets, and S.138 cases make up a large share of that load.

| Route | Realistic Timeline |
|---|---|
| Full contested trial | 2–4 years (Magistrate to Sessions appeal) |
| Early compounding | Weeks to a few months (1–4 hearings) |
| Strong notice-stage defence | Often resolved within 6–12 months |

The strategic lesson is clear: if you are the accused and the debt is genuine, compound early and avoid a conviction. If you are the complainant, a clean procedural record gives you strong leverage for early and favourable settlement. Both sides benefit from resolving before the full trial machine runs its course.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: I received a legal notice for cheque bounce. What do I do immediately?

Do not ignore the notice and do not let the 15-day grace period expire without taking action. Verify the cheque details, the dishonour date, and when you received the notice. If you can pay the full cheque amount within 15 days of receiving the notice, do so and keep documented proof — this prevents a complaint from being filed entirely. If payment is not possible, consult a lawyer immediately to assess your defences and begin settlement negotiations. A carefully worded reply to the notice can also be sent, but must be drafted so that it does not inadvertently amount to an admission of liability.

### Q: Can I be arrested for cheque bounce?

Yes, technically — but arrest in S.138 cases is far less common than most people fear. The court first issues a summons, and you are expected to appear voluntarily. Arrest typically happens only when an accused repeatedly fails to appear, triggering a non-bailable warrant. If you appear on the summons date and apply for bail, it will almost certainly be granted. The key is not to ignore court notices — non-appearance escalates a manageable situation into a much more serious one very quickly.

### Q: My company's cheque bounced — am I personally liable as a director?

Not automatically. Under Section 141 NI Act, personal liability attaches only to directors who were "in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business" when the offence occurred. A nominee director, a director who resigned before the cheque was issued, or a director with no executive role in financial operations has a strong defence. This defence must be pleaded specifically at the earliest stage and supported by documentary evidence — board resolutions, appointment terms, or resignation letters. Do not assume that being a director in name makes you equally liable.

### Q: The cheque was given as security, not for a debt. Is this a complete defence?

It is a very strong defence, but you must prove it. Under S.139 NI Act, once the complainant establishes that the cheque was issued and dishonoured, the court presumes it was for a legally enforceable debt. To rebut this, you must lead positive evidence — written agreements, email exchanges, WhatsApp conversations, or witness testimony establishing the security nature of the arrangement. Courts in Pune have acquitted accused persons on this ground where the security nature of the cheque was well-documented from the beginning.

### Q: How long does a cheque bounce case take in Pune's courts?

A fully contested trial in Pune's JMFC courts typically takes two to four years from complaint to judgment. Early compounding — where both parties agree to settle — can close the matter in weeks to a few months, and is almost always the commercially sensible option where the debt is genuine. If you are fighting on a legitimate legal defence (no debt, defective notice, limitation), trials do run long, but strong preliminary applications can significantly reduce the practical burden on the accused during pendency.
