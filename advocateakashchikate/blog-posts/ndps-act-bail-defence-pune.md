# NDPS Act in Pune: Bail, Section 37, and Your Defence Options (2026)

An NDPS arrest is one of the most frightening things a person or their family can face in India. The Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985 carries severe minimum sentences, imposes a reverse burden of proof in many situations, and — critically — makes bail far harder to obtain than in almost any other criminal case.

This guide gives you an honest picture of what you are facing, what the law actually says, and where the genuine opportunities for defence lie.

Bail in NDPS cases is difficult. It is not impossible. The cases that go wrong are usually the ones where no serious legal challenge was mounted from the start — where the bail application was treated as a formality rather than as the first act of a defence strategy.

If someone you know has been arrested under the NDPS Act in Pune, the time to act is now. Every hour in police custody before a lawyer appears increases pressure on the accused to make statements that will later be difficult to retract.

---

## What the NDPS Act Covers

The NDPS Act criminalises the production, manufacture, possession, sale, purchase, transport, use, and financing of narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances. Its reach is broader than most people assume.

The most commonly encountered substances in Pune NDPS cases are cannabis (ganja, charas, and hashish), heroin, cocaine, and increasingly, synthetic and party drugs — MDMA, methamphetamine, and similar scheduled substances. The IT corridor and nightlife areas of Pune see a distinct pattern of cases compared to transit or rural seizure cases.

What surprises many people is that the NDPS Act also covers certain prescription medications. Sedatives, strong painkillers, and some anti-anxiety medications are scheduled under the Act. Possession of these substances without a valid prescription — or in quantities exceeding what a prescription would justify — can attract NDPS charges. Prosecutions in Pune under these provisions are not uncommon, particularly following vehicle checks.

The Act targets those who possess, transit, trade in, or finance the drug trade. Being present near drugs, having consumed drugs, or being connected to someone involved does not automatically make a person liable — but it very often leads to arrest while the investigation establishes culpability.

The distinction between use, possession, and trafficking is fundamental. It determines the charge, the sentencing exposure, and the bail threshold. This is the first question any competent NDPS lawyer must answer.

## Why Quantities Matter Enormously

The quantity of the substance allegedly recovered is the single most important factor in determining the charge filed, the minimum sentence applicable, and how difficult bail will be. This is not an overstatement.

Under the NDPS Act, substances are categorised as involving small quantity, intermediate quantity, or commercial quantity. The thresholds are substance-specific and set by government notification.

| Substance | Small Quantity | Commercial Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Cannabis (Ganja) | Up to 1 kg | 20 kg or more |
| Charas / Hashish | Up to 100 g | 1 kg or more |
| Heroin | Up to 5 g | 250 g or more |
| Cocaine | Up to 2 g | 100 g or more |
| MDMA | Up to 0.5 g | 10 g or more |

For small quantity possession, the maximum sentence is six months to one year and bail conditions are relatively standard. For commercial quantity — or for financing the drug trade, which carries commercial quantity consequences regardless of the physical amount seized — the mandatory minimum sentence is ten years, extendable to twenty years, with a fine of up to one lakh rupees.

More importantly for the immediate situation, commercial quantity triggers Section 37, which makes bail dramatically harder to obtain. The intermediate zone — between small and commercial — carries sentences of up to ten years without the mandatory minimum, and bail is assessed under more ordinary legal standards.

## Section 37 — The Bail Threshold Explained

Section 37 of the NDPS Act is the provision that makes NDPS bail categorically different from bail in virtually every other criminal case in India. It applies to offences punishable with five or more years, which includes commercial quantity cases and all trafficking-related charges.

Under Section 37, a court cannot grant bail unless two conditions are met: first, there are reasonable grounds to believe the accused is not guilty of the offence charged; and second, the accused is unlikely to commit any further offence while on bail. This is a reverse burden — in ordinary criminal law, the accused is presumed innocent and bail is the norm. Under Section 37, the court must be affirmatively satisfied of both these matters before granting any bail at all.

A bare denial of guilt is not enough to secure bail in a commercial NDPS case. The defence must present material — at the bail stage itself — that gives the court a reason to believe the accused may not be guilty. This requires careful preparation of the bail application, not a routine filing.

**What strengthens an NDPS bail application in Pune courts:**

A first-time offender with no prior criminal record carries significant weight. Courts are more willing to hold that such a person was not the principal actor, and the likelihood of future offending is lower. The accused being present at a location where drugs were recovered, but not being the primary possessor, is a distinct point — mere presence is not possession.

Strong community ties — family in Pune, stable employment, owned property, long residence — address the flight risk and reoffending elements directly. Medical conditions requiring ongoing treatment, or extended time already spent in custody relative to the likely sentence, are also regularly relied upon. Procedural flaws in the recovery process are the most powerful basis of all — where the seizure itself is challengeable, the court has genuine grounds to doubt guilt.

## Challenging the Evidence — Where NDPS Cases Collapse

The NDPS Act and its rules impose very specific procedures on officers conducting searches and seizures. These requirements exist precisely because the consequences of conviction are so severe. In practice, they are frequently not followed — and violations create genuine legal defences.

**The independent witness (panch) requirement.** Under Sections 42 and 43 of the NDPS Act, searches must be conducted in the presence of independent witnesses. Officers frequently use other police personnel as panchas, or the same witnesses appear in multiple unrelated cases — both are procedural violations. Where the panch witness cannot be produced for cross-examination, or where their testimony contradicts the police version of events, the recovery itself may become unreliable.

**Weighing the seized material in the accused's presence.** The entire recovered substance must be weighed in the accused's presence, and the sampling procedure for FSL testing must follow specific rules. If weighing was done without the accused present, or if the sample sent for testing was not properly sealed and documented at the point of seizure, the chain of custody is broken.

**FSL (Forensic Science Lab) report gaps.** The substance must be sent to the FSL, tested, and the report produced in court. Delays, missing seals, improper storage between seizure and testing, and discrepancies between the quantity recovered and the quantity tested are all issues that defence lawyers in Pune have raised successfully at both the bail and trial stages.

**Planted substance allegations.** Not every NDPS case involves substances that genuinely belonged to the accused. Cases of planting occur, and Pune has seen acquittals based on this defence. The credibility of recovery witnesses, the circumstances of the search, any inconsistencies in the police story, and the accused's background and relationship with the seizing officers are all relevant.

**Recovery from common areas.** Substances recovered from a vehicle carrying multiple passengers, a shared room, a common building area, or luggage with multiple owners cannot automatically be attributed to any individual without further evidence of possession or knowledge. This is a live issue in a significant proportion of Pune NDPS cases.

## Key Judgments — What Courts Have Said

**Tofan Singh v. State of Tamil Nadu (Supreme Court, 2021):** This is the most important NDPS defence judgment in recent years. The Supreme Court held that NCB and Customs officers are "police officers" for the purposes of the Evidence Act, and therefore statements made to them are inadmissible under Section 25 of the Indian Evidence Act (now Section 23, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam). In cases where the prosecution's primary evidence is a confession made to the NCB, Tofan Singh is directly applicable and often devastating.

**Sujit Tiwari v. State of Gujarat (Supreme Court, 2019):** The Supreme Court reiterated that Section 37 does not make bail categorically impossible — it requires courts to consider individual circumstances. Extended custody, no trial date in sight, and particular facts of the case can support bail even in commercial quantity matters. This judgment is the foundation for arguing "prolonged custody" bail where trial is unlikely to conclude for years.

**Bombay High Court on Section 42 procedure violations:** The Bombay HC has a consistent line of authority holding that material violations of the mandatory procedural requirements in Sections 41, 42, and 43 of the NDPS Act result in the entire recovery being tainted. Acquittals in Pune NDPS matters have been sustained by the High Court on exactly this basis. These judgments are not obscure — they are directly applicable arguments in many active cases.

## What Happens After Arrest — The Practical Timeline

Understanding the procedural timeline helps families make decisions quickly and reduces the panic that leads to bad choices.

**Day 1–2 — Police custody.** The arrested person is in police custody. Under BNSS, police custody can be authorised for up to 15 days, applied for and approved by a Magistrate. This is the period of greatest pressure — when statements are sought and conditions are most difficult. A lawyer appearing at the first production hearing can limit the period of police custody remand and ensure the accused is not questioned alone.

**After police custody — Judicial custody.** The accused is remanded to judicial custody and typically sent to Yerawada Central Prison, which handles the majority of Pune's NDPS under-trial population. Judicial custody continues until bail is granted or the trial concludes.

**Bail hearing.** A bail application can typically be listed for hearing at the Pune Sessions Court within one to two weeks of filing. If the first application is rejected at Sessions Court, a fresh application is filed before the Bombay High Court. High Court NDPS bail matters are typically listed within two to four weeks; urgent listing can be sought where circumstances justify it.

**Trial.** NDPS trials in Pune typically take between three and seven years, depending on the number of accused persons, the volume of documents, and the FSL report timeline. This protracted process is itself a strong argument for bail — keeping someone in custody for seven years while a case proceeds effectively negates the presumption of innocence. Courts are increasingly receptive to this argument.

## ANC Cases in Pune — The Anti-Narcotics Cell and How It Operates

Most NDPS cases in Pune are handled by the Pune Police's Anti-Narcotics Cell (ANC), which operates from Shivajinagar. The ANC conducts its own intelligence-based operations as well as assisting other police stations when drugs are recovered incidentally during unrelated investigations.

Understanding which agency is handling your case matters legally. ANC cases follow the standard NDPS Act procedure — search, seizure, arrest, FSL testing. NCB (Narcotics Control Bureau) cases involve the central government's enforcement agency and have some different procedural characteristics, including the use of Section 67 NDPS statements, which were addressed by the Supreme Court in Tofan Singh.

The ANC in Pune has historically focused on mid-level supply chain cases — suppliers, couriers, and distributors — as well as party drug seizures and cases arising from tipoffs at check-posts and nightlife venues. End users caught with small quantities are often dealt with at the police station level, not by the ANC directly, and the approach in practice reflects the quantity and apparent purpose of possession.

When the ANC investigates a case, the investigating officer typically applies for police custody to conduct a sustained interrogation and identify the supply chain. This is the period of maximum vulnerability for an accused person, and legal representation at the first production is critical.

**Surrender is not the same as cooperation.** If you hear that the ANC wants to "talk" to you, do not present yourself without consulting a criminal lawyer first. What the ANC describes as a conversation may result in arrest at the Cyber Cell premises. In NDPS cases, the consequences of arrest without a bail strategy in place are severe — Yerawada remand, no bail for weeks, and statements made under pressure.

If there is credible information that the ANC is investigating you or intends to arrest you, this is precisely the situation for which anticipatory bail exists. The application should be filed immediately, before any arrest takes place.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: I was found in a car where drugs were discovered. Am I automatically liable?**
A: No. Presence in a vehicle at the time of a drug seizure does not equal possession. The prosecution must prove you knew about the drugs and had some degree of control over them. Where there are multiple occupants, the question of who the drugs belonged to is a genuine one that must be established through evidence — it is not resolved simply by being in the vehicle. Courts in Maharashtra have acquitted passengers in such cases where there was no evidence beyond presence.

**Q: Can first-time NDPS offenders in Pune get bail?**
A: Yes, first-time offenders have the best chance under the Section 37 framework. The absence of a prior record is the primary factor demonstrating that the accused is unlikely to commit further offences while on bail. Combined with strong family ties, stable employment, and any procedural flaws in the case, bail applications by first-time offenders in small or intermediate quantity matters have a reasonable prospect of success. Even in commercial quantity cases, first-time status is a material advantage at the bail hearing.

**Q: I was using drugs but not selling. Does that matter for bail?**
A: It can matter significantly. Personal use involving small quantities attracts far lighter consequences than possession for sale or transit. If the quantity recovered is within the small quantity threshold for that substance, Section 37 does not apply at all — the bail threshold reverts to ordinary criminal law standards. The prosecution will typically allege possession for sale whenever the quantity exceeds small quantity, but this allegation must be proved, not assumed. Challenging the characterisation of purpose of possession is a core part of NDPS bail strategy.

**Q: Are there treatment or rehabilitation alternatives to imprisonment for drug users?**
A: Yes. Section 64A of the NDPS Act provides that an addict who voluntarily seeks treatment at a recognised centre and successfully completes it may be immunised from prosecution for personal use offences. This provision is genuinely underused in Pune. Additionally, courts at the sentencing stage have the power to direct that a first-time offender with genuine dependence issues be sent to a treatment facility rather than prison, particularly where personal use quantities are involved. These options are worth discussing with your lawyer from the outset — not after conviction.

**Q: My family member was arrested late at night. What should we do right now?**
A: First, find out which police station made the arrest and confirm they have been produced before a Magistrate (this must happen within 24 hours). Contact a criminal lawyer immediately — the first production hearing is when the initial remand period is determined, and legal representation at this stage is critical. Gather identity and address documents, employment proof, and details of property or family ties in Pune — these will be needed for the bail application. Do not attempt to negotiate with police officers directly or offer anything to secure release. Everything should go through your lawyer.

**Q: Can bail conditions be relaxed after they are imposed?**
A: Yes. Bail conditions — such as a requirement to report daily to a police station, surrender of passport, or restriction on travel — can be modified by application to the court that granted bail. Where conditions are disproportionately burdensome given the circumstances, a modification application regularly succeeds. For example, a daily reporting condition can often be reduced to weekly reporting where the accused has no prior violations and has complied consistently. If a passport is needed for legitimate work travel while on bail, an application can be made to temporarily lift the surrender condition.
