## What Default Bail Is

Default bail is the right to be released from custody if the police fail to file a chargesheet (the formal document placing the case before a court) within the time limits set by law.

It is not granted because the accused is innocent. It is not granted because the court feels sympathetic. It is granted because the law says: the state cannot hold a person in jail indefinitely while police take their time completing an investigation. There is a deadline. If that deadline passes and no chargesheet is filed, the accused has an indefeasible right — meaning a right that cannot be taken away — to bail.

This right exists under **Section 479 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)**, which replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. Under the old law, this provision was **Section 167(2) CrPC**. Both names appear in legal documents, depending on when the case was filed. The right itself is the same.

The Supreme Court of India has confirmed the nature of this right repeatedly. In the landmark case of Rakesh Kumar Paul v. State of Assam, the Supreme Court made clear that once the deadline passes and no chargesheet has been filed, the right to default bail is absolute. The court cannot refuse it. The Magistrate cannot exercise discretion and say "but the case is serious." Seriousness is irrelevant. The law set a deadline, the state missed it, and the right belongs to the accused.

## The Exact Deadlines You Need to Know

Under Section 187(3) BNSS, the deadlines are:

- **90 days** from the date of arrest, for offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment of ten years or more.
- **60 days** from the date of arrest, for all other offences.

Count from the **date of arrest** — not the date the Magistrate first heard the bail application, not the date remand was granted. The clock starts the day the person was taken into custody.

If the 60th or 90th day passes — depending on the offence — and no chargesheet has been filed in court, the accused has the right to apply for default bail immediately.

---

## The Critical Warning: This Right Evaporates the Moment the Chargesheet is Filed

Here is the fact that makes speed non-negotiable.

The right to default bail disappears the instant the police file the chargesheet — even if it is filed on day 89 of a 90-day deadline. Even if it is filed on the last possible hour. If the chargesheet is filed before the default bail application is moved in court, the right is gone. It cannot be revived.

This is not a technicality. This is the settled legal position. In practice, it means that once you believe the deadline has passed, you must contact a criminal defence lawyer and file the application that same day. Do not wait for a holiday to pass. Do not wait for a hearing that is scheduled for next week. The chargesheet could be filed tonight.

Urgency here is not dramatic language. It is the literal structure of the law.

## How to Apply for Default Bail

The accused, or their lawyer on their behalf, files an application before the Magistrate who has jurisdiction over the case — typically the same Magistrate before whom the accused is being produced for remand hearings.

The application states that: the accused has been in custody since a specific date; the offence falls under the 60-day or 90-day category; that deadline has now passed; and no chargesheet has been filed. The accused therefore invokes their right under Section 187(3) BNSS and requests to be released on bail.

The Magistrate is required to grant this bail if the facts are as stated. It is not a matter of the court's discretion about flight risk, danger to society, or the gravity of the offence. Those considerations apply to regular bail. Default bail is different. The law grants it as a right, and the Magistrate's role is to verify that the deadline has passed and the chargesheet has not been filed.

---

## What If the Magistrate Refuses?

If a Magistrate refuses to grant default bail despite the deadline having passed and no chargesheet being filed, that refusal is wrong in law. It can and should be challenged.

The next step is an immediate application before the Sessions Court in Pune. If the Sessions Court also refuses, the matter goes to the Bombay High Court. Courts at the higher level routinely correct such refusals because the law is clear on this point.

Do not accept a refusal as the final word without getting a second legal opinion.

## Common Situations Where Default Bail Applies in Pune

**NDPS (drug) cases:** Offences under the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act that carry 10 years or more imprisonment fall under the 60-day deadline. Investigations in NDPS cases often take time because they involve forensic analysis and multiple accused persons. The 60-day deadline is frequently missed. Yet many families in Pune with a member in custody for an NDPS case do not know this right exists.

**Section 498A cases (matrimonial cruelty):** Section 498A (BNS Section 85) carries a maximum sentence of 3 years — so the 60-day deadline applies. Where the accused is in judicial custody and the investigation drags past 60 days without a chargesheet, default bail applies. These cases sometimes involve complex family disputes where police take time gathering statements. Note: if additional sections carrying 10+ years are charged alongside 498A, the 90-day deadline applies instead.

**Cheating and economic offence cases:** Investigations into financial fraud, property cheating, or corporate disputes frequently take months. Where the accused is in custody and the 60 or 90-day deadline passes, the right to default bail arises.

## How Default Bail is Different from Regular Bail

This distinction is important.

When a lawyer applies for regular bail, the court weighs multiple factors: the nature of the offence, whether the accused is a flight risk, whether the accused might tamper with evidence, criminal history, and so on. The court has discretion. It can refuse. It often does refuse in serious cases.

Default bail does not go through that analysis. The court asks only two questions: Has the deadline passed? Has the chargesheet been filed? If the answer is yes to the first and no to the second, the bail must be granted. Prior refusal of regular bail does not bar an application for default bail. The two are completely independent of each other.

---

## What Conditions Come With Default Bail

Default bail is bail — not unconditional release. The Magistrate will typically impose conditions: a personal bond and one or two sureties (people who guarantee the accused will attend court), reporting to the police station on specified days, surrender of passport, and a restriction on leaving the jurisdiction without court permission.

These are standard. They are not punishments. Comply with them carefully, because violation of bail conditions can result in the bail being cancelled.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do I know if a chargesheet has been filed yet?

The most reliable way is to have a lawyer check the court record in the relevant Magistrate's court in Pune. Chargesheets are filed in court and entered in the court register — a lawyer can confirm this within a day. The accused's family can also ask the jail authorities whether a chargesheet has been filed and the case number, though this is not always reliable. Do not guess. Have a lawyer verify it formally.

### Q: Can I apply for default bail if regular bail was already rejected?

Yes. A previous refusal of regular bail has no bearing on a default bail application. They are completely separate legal rights. Regular bail is a matter of the court's discretion; default bail is a statutory right under Section 187(3) BNSS. The court cannot refuse default bail on the ground that regular bail was denied.

### Q: What if the Magistrate says the deadline hasn't passed yet?

Count the days yourself and have your lawyer count them independently. The deadline runs from the date of arrest — confirm this date from the FIR and the Magistrate's first remand order. If your count shows the deadline has passed, your lawyer should place this on record before the Magistrate with the supporting documents. If there is a genuine dispute about dates, the court record and the FIR will be the reference points. If the Magistrate's count is simply wrong, the Sessions Court or High Court can be approached immediately for correction.

### Q: Does default bail apply in NDPS / drug cases?

Yes. NDPS offences carrying 10 years or more fall under the **90-day** deadline under Section 187(3) BNSS. NDPS investigations routinely take more than 90 days, making this one of the most common situations where default bail becomes available in Pune. NDPS cases do have special provisions under the NDPS Act that make regular bail harder to obtain — but those provisions do not override the default bail right. The Supreme Court has confirmed that the right under Section 167(2) CrPC (now Section 187(3) BNSS) applies to NDPS cases.
