# Cybercrime FIR in Pune: What You Must Do in the First 24 Hours (2026)

Most people who receive a cybercrime notice or learn that an FIR has been registered against them make at least one critical mistake in the first 24 hours — and that mistake can define the outcome of their entire case. The good news is that these errors are entirely avoidable if you know what to do.

This guide is written for anyone in Pune who has received a notice from the Cyber Cell, been told by someone that a complaint has been filed against them, or already knows an FIR exists. Read this before you do anything else.

---

## What NOT to Do (Common Mistakes That Destroy Cases)

These are the actions that most harm accused persons — and they happen almost universally because people act on panic rather than legal advice.

**Do not contact the complainant.** This is the single most damaging thing you can do. Even if your intention is to explain the situation or reach a settlement, any communication with the complainant after an FIR is registered can be used as evidence of consciousness of guilt, intimidation, or tampering with a witness. Courts take a very dim view of this. If you know the complainant personally, resist every instinct to reach out.

**Do not delete any data from your devices.** Deleting emails, WhatsApp messages, photographs, or any other digital material after becoming aware of an FIR or notice is a separate criminal offence — evidence tampering under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita. Even if the data seems embarrassing or irrelevant, do not touch it. Courts consistently treat deletion as an implied admission of guilt.

**Do not give any statement to the police without a lawyer present.** You have a constitutional right under Article 20(3) not to be compelled to be a witness against yourself. Police may approach you informally, call you to the station "just to talk," or suggest that cooperating without a lawyer shows good faith. None of this is true. Anything you say without legal representation can be recorded and used against you.

**Do not explain yourself on social media.** It feels natural to want to tell your side of the story — to friends, family, or publicly. Do not do this. Social media posts after an FIR are routinely printed and produced as exhibits by prosecution. Your post intended to show innocence may instead be read as knowledge of the allegations or an attempt to influence the narrative.

**Do not pay anyone to "make it go away."** There are individuals — sometimes posing as fixers, sometimes as intermediaries connected to the complainant — who approach accused persons in cybercrime cases and offer to get the FIR withdrawn for money. Paying such persons is not only futile but can itself amount to bribery or abetment of extortion, and has been used in several Pune cases as evidence of guilt by the prosecution. Any legitimate settlement happens only through proper legal channels.

**Do not voluntarily surrender your devices without understanding your rights.** If police arrive and ask you to hand over your phone, laptop, or hard drive, they need either your consent or a lawful search warrant. You may be pressured to "cooperate." Call your lawyer first — device seizures done without following due process are challengeable, and their outcome affects everything that follows.

## What to Do Immediately

These four steps, taken in the right order, protect your rights and give your lawyer the best possible foundation to work from.

**Step 1 — Note the details.** If you have received a physical notice, record the police station name, the case or complaint number, the date of the notice, and the name of the officer who signed it. If someone has merely called you or told you about an FIR, note the date and what was said. Everything begins here.

**Step 2 — Preserve all related communications.** Take screenshots of every relevant email, WhatsApp conversation, text message, or social media interaction that connects you (or does not connect you) to the alleged offence. Back these up to a secure cloud location. This evidence is often the strongest tool for the defence, but only if it is preserved before the devices are examined or seized.

**Step 3 — Preserve all devices in their current state.** Do not factory reset your phone. Do not format your laptop. Do not transfer files. The current state of your device is potential evidence — both for and against you. A lawyer and digital forensic expert can advise on exactly what to preserve and how. Tampering with devices after receiving a notice is one of the most common and damaging mistakes made by accused persons in Pune cybercrime cases.

**Step 4 — Call a criminal lawyer before responding to anything.** Before you appear at the Cyber Cell, before you reply to the notice, and before you speak to anyone connected with the case — speak to a lawyer. At this stage, the case can still move in many different directions. Early legal intervention often prevents arrests, secures anticipatory bail, and identifies procedural flaws in the FIR that can be used at the trial stage or in a quashing petition.

## Is It a Notice or an FIR? — The Crucial Difference

The nature of the document you have received determines both your immediate legal position and what remedies are available to you. This is a distinction most people do not understand, and it matters enormously.

**Section 160 BNSS Notice (Witness Notice):** This notice requires you to appear before the investigating officer as a witness — not as an accused. You may be required to appear and answer questions, but you cannot be arrested on the basis of this notice alone, and your right against self-incrimination still applies to questions that might implicate you. However, do not attend without a lawyer. Many witness notices in cybercrime cases are effectively fishing expeditions by investigators trying to build a case against someone they do not yet have enough evidence to charge.

**Accused Notice:** This means you are named as a suspect or accused in the investigation. The moment you are named as an accused, your right against self-incrimination is fully engaged — you have no obligation to answer questions that might incriminate you. Do not appear without legal representation. The police may present this as a routine or informal step, but it is not. This is the stage at which your defence begins.

**FIR — First Information Report:** An FIR means a formal criminal case has been registered. The police are now legally obligated to investigate and may arrest you. If the offence is cognizable (which most cybercrime offences under the IT Act and BNS are), the police can arrest without a warrant. This is the stage at which bail risk becomes real and anticipatory bail should be considered immediately.

To tell which category a Cyber Cell notice falls into, look for the word "accused," "suspect," or the section numbers of the offences cited. If sections from the BNS or IT Act are listed alongside your name, treat it as an accused notice, not a witness notice, and seek legal advice immediately.

## When Is Anticipatory Bail Needed?

Anticipatory bail is an order from the Sessions Court or High Court that protects you from arrest. Once granted, police cannot take you into custody without first producing you before that court.

You should seek anticipatory bail urgently in these situations: if you are named as an accused in an FIR; if a Cyber Cell notice describes you as a suspect or uses language suggesting you are the target of the investigation; if the investigating officer has indicated (formally or informally) that arrest is likely; or if the nature of the offence and the amounts involved suggest that police will take an active approach to the case.

The practical advantage of anticipatory bail in cybercrime cases is significant. It allows you to participate in the investigation on your own terms, attend the Cyber Cell with legal representation, and cooperate with the investigation without the risk of being held in police custody — where the pressure to make statements is enormous.

Anticipatory bail applications in Pune cybercrime matters can typically be filed within 24–48 hours of instruction. Interim protection from arrest is frequently granted at the first hearing itself, giving the court time to hear the full application.

## The "Money Mule" Situation — A Common and Winnable Defence

One of the most frequently seen cybercrime FIR scenarios in Pune is what is called a "money mule" case. Your bank account was used to receive or transfer money that turns out to be proceeds of an online fraud — without your knowledge. Banks report these accounts to the Cyber Cell, the Cyber Cell traces the account holder, and you find yourself named in an FIR you had nothing to do with.

How it happens: fraudsters convince individuals to lend their bank accounts, open accounts in exchange for payment, or simply use compromised account credentials to route stolen money. The account holder is often completely unaware. When the fraud is investigated, the trail leads to the last known account — yours.

The legal defence in these cases is strong: there is no mens rea (criminal intent). Possession of an account used in a fraud chain, without knowledge of the fraud, is not a criminal offence. The prosecution must prove that you knowingly participated in the scheme. Courts in Maharashtra have regularly accepted this defence where it is supported by evidence of normal account usage patterns, no unusual cash withdrawals, and no other connection to the fraudsters.

What you must not do if this happens: do not withdraw cash, do not transfer any remaining balance, and do not follow any instructions from people claiming to be "resolving the issue" for you — these are the fraudsters trying to move money before the investigation catches up. Each of those actions undermines your defence.

This is a genuinely winnable situation when handled correctly and from the outset.

## Common Cybercrime FIR Scenarios in Pune

**IT professional accused by former employer:** Allegations of data theft, unauthorised access to systems, or breach of confidentiality under the IT Act and BNS. These cases are often driven by corporate disputes rather than genuine criminal conduct. The key defence turns on access logs, employment agreements, and whether the accused's actions fell within authorised use.

**OTP fraud where the SIM was compromised:** The accused's phone number or SIM was used in a fraud without their knowledge — through SIM swap fraud, phishing, or malware. The victim reports the number, and the number's registered owner is named. Establishing the SIM compromise is the foundation of this defence.

**Social media abuse and defamation complaints:** A post, comment, forward, or meme results in a complaint under Section 351 BNS (criminal intimidation), Section 356 BNS (defamation), or IT Act provisions. Context, intent, and whether the content was actually created or merely forwarded are all relevant.

**Online trading fraud where you are wrongly named:** You invested in a scheme and lost money — but the complaint has been structured so that participants are named alongside operators. Distinguishing investors from perpetrators is the primary legal task in these cases.

**Data theft allegations in corporate disputes:** An employer claims an employee took client lists, source code, or confidential files. These cases frequently arise from legitimate exits gone wrong, and the evidence often falls apart under scrutiny of what was actually taken versus what was authorised.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do I have to go to the cyber cell if they call me?**
A: It depends entirely on the nature of the notice or call. A formal Section 160 BNSS notice to appear as a witness creates a legal obligation to appear, but you may seek exemption from personal appearance or send a representative in certain circumstances. An informal phone call creates no legal obligation whatsoever. Before going to the Cyber Cell for any reason, speak to a lawyer first. Going without legal advice — even as a witness — can result in your status being converted to that of an accused on the same day.

**Q: Can police arrest me without warning in a cybercrime case?**
A: Yes. Most cybercrime offences under the IT Act and BNS are cognizable offences, meaning police can arrest without a warrant. They do not need to warn you in advance. This is exactly why anticipatory bail is so valuable in these cases — it prevents arrest before you have had a chance to present your side.

**Q: What if I genuinely do not know why I received the notice?**
A: This is more common than most people realise. The notice may have been sent to you because your phone number, email address, bank account, or IP address appeared in an investigation without your direct involvement. Do not assume the notice is a mistake and ignore it. Engage a lawyer who can contact the investigating officer formally and establish the basis for the notice before you decide how to respond.

**Q: How long do cybercrime investigations in Pune take before charges are filed?**
A: Under BNSS, police must file a charge sheet within 60 days of arrest for most offences (extendable to 90 days for serious offences). If you are not arrested but are under investigation, there is no strict deadline for filing the charge sheet. Investigations often take six months to two years before charges are formally filed. This is another reason early legal intervention matters — the pre-charge period is often when cases can be most effectively resolved or weakened.
