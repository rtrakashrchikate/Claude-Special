# Cybercrime FIR in Pune: What You Must Do in the First 24 Hours (2026)

Most people who receive a cybercrime notice or learn that an FIR has been registered against them make at least one critical mistake in the first 24 hours — and that mistake can define the outcome of their entire case.

The good news is that these errors are entirely avoidable if you know what to do. This guide is written for anyone in Pune who has received a Cyber Cell notice, been told by someone that a complaint has been filed, or already knows an FIR exists. Read this before you do anything else.

## What NOT to Do — Common Mistakes That Destroy Cases

These are the actions that most harm accused persons. They happen almost universally because people act on panic rather than legal advice.

**Do not contact the complainant.** This is the single most damaging thing you can do. Even if your intention is to explain the situation or reach a settlement, any communication with the complainant after an FIR is registered can be used as evidence of consciousness of guilt, intimidation, or witness tampering. Courts take a very dim view of it.

**Do not delete any data from your devices.** Deleting emails, WhatsApp messages, photos, or any other digital material after becoming aware of an FIR or notice is a separate criminal offence — evidence tampering under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita. Even if the data seems embarrassing or irrelevant to you, do not touch it. Courts consistently treat deletion as an implied admission of guilt.

**Do not give any statement to the police without a lawyer present.** You have a constitutional right under Article 20(3) not to be compelled to be a witness against yourself. Police may approach you informally, call you to the station "just to talk," or suggest that cooperating without a lawyer shows good faith. None of this is true.

**Do not explain yourself on social media.** It feels natural to want to tell your side of the story — to friends, family, or publicly. Do not do this. Social media posts made after an FIR are routinely printed and produced as exhibits by the prosecution. A post intended to show your innocence may instead be read as knowledge of the allegations or an attempt to influence the narrative.

**Do not pay anyone to "make it go away."** There are individuals who approach accused persons in cybercrime cases and offer to get the FIR withdrawn for money — sometimes posing as fixers, sometimes as intermediaries connected to the complainant. Paying such persons is not only futile but can itself amount to bribery. In several Pune cases, such payments have been used by the prosecution as admissions of guilt.

**Do not voluntarily surrender your devices without understanding your rights.** If police arrive and ask you to hand over your phone, laptop, or hard drive, they need either your consent or a lawful search warrant. You may be pressured to "cooperate." Call your lawyer first. Device seizures conducted without following due process are challengeable — and the outcome of the device examination affects everything that follows.

## What to Do Immediately

These four steps, taken in the right order, protect your rights and give your lawyer the best possible foundation to work from.

**Step 1 — Note the details from the notice.** Record the police station name, the case or complaint number, the date of the notice, and the name of the officer who signed it. If someone merely called you or told you informally about a case, note the date and what was said. Everything begins here.

**Step 2 — Screenshot and preserve all related communications.** Take screenshots of every relevant email, WhatsApp conversation, text message, or social media interaction that connects you (or does not connect you) to the alleged offence. Back these up to a secure cloud location. This evidence is often the strongest tool for the defence — but only if it is preserved before devices are examined or seized.

**Step 3 — Preserve all devices in their current state.** Do not factory reset your phone. Do not format your laptop. Do not transfer files between devices. The current state of your device is potential evidence — both for and against you. A lawyer and digital forensic expert can advise on exactly what to preserve and how.

**Step 4 — Call a criminal lawyer before responding to anything.** Before you appear at the Cyber Cell, before you reply to the notice, before you speak to anyone connected with the case — speak to a lawyer. At this early stage, the case can still move in many directions. Early legal intervention often prevents arrests, secures anticipatory bail, and identifies procedural flaws in the FIR that can be used later at trial or in a quashing petition.

## Is It a Notice or an FIR? — The Crucial Difference

The nature of the document you have received determines both your immediate legal position and the remedies available to you. Most people do not understand this distinction, and it matters enormously.

**Section 160 BNSS Notice (Witness Notice):** This notice requires you to appear before the investigating officer as a witness — not as an accused. You may be required to appear and answer questions, but you cannot be arrested on the basis of this notice alone, and your right against self-incrimination applies to any questions that might implicate you personally.

Do not attend without a lawyer. Many witness notices in cybercrime cases are effectively fishing expeditions — investigators trying to build a case against someone they do not yet have enough evidence to charge. Your attendance without counsel can rapidly change your status.

**Accused Notice:** This means you are named as a suspect in the investigation. The moment you are named as an accused, your right against self-incrimination is fully engaged — you have no obligation to answer questions that might incriminate you. Do not appear without legal representation. The police may present this as routine, but it is not. This is the stage at which your defence begins.

**FIR — First Information Report:** An FIR means a formal criminal case has been registered. The police are now legally obligated to investigate and may arrest you. Most cybercrime offences under the IT Act and BNS are cognizable — meaning police can arrest without a warrant. This is the stage at which bail risk becomes real and anticipatory bail should be considered immediately.

To tell which category a Cyber Cell notice falls into, look for the word "accused," "suspect," or the section numbers of offences cited alongside your name. If BNS or IT Act sections are cited with your name, treat it as an accused notice — not a witness notice — and seek legal advice at once.

## When Is Anticipatory Bail Needed?

Anticipatory bail is an order from the Sessions Court or High Court that protects you from arrest. Once granted, police cannot take you into custody without first producing you before that court.

You should seek anticipatory bail urgently in these situations: if you are named as an accused in an FIR; if a Cyber Cell notice describes you as a "suspect" or uses language suggesting you are the target of the investigation; if the investigating officer has indicated (formally or informally) that arrest is likely; or if the nature of the offence and amounts involved suggest police will take an active approach.

The practical advantage of anticipatory bail in cybercrime cases is significant. It allows you to participate in the investigation on your own terms, attend the Cyber Cell with legal representation, and cooperate with investigators without the risk of being held in police custody — where the pressure to make statements is enormous.

Anticipatory bail applications in Pune cybercrime matters can typically be filed within 24–48 hours of instruction. Interim protection from arrest is frequently granted at the first hearing itself, giving the court time to hear the full application without the accused being in custody in the meantime.

## The "Money Mule" Situation — A Common and Winnable Defence

One of the most frequently seen cybercrime FIR scenarios in Pune is the "money mule" case. Your bank account was used to receive or transfer money that turns out to be proceeds of an online fraud — without your knowledge. Banks report these accounts to the Cyber Cell; the Cyber Cell traces the account holder; and you find yourself named in an FIR you had nothing to do with.

How it happens: fraudsters convince individuals to lend their bank accounts, open new accounts in exchange for payment, or simply use compromised credentials to route stolen money. The account holder is often completely unaware. When the fraud is investigated, the trail leads to the last known account — yours.

The legal defence in these cases is strong. There is no mens rea — no criminal intent. Possession of an account used in a fraud chain, without knowledge of the fraud, is not a criminal offence. The prosecution must prove that you knowingly participated in the scheme. Courts in Maharashtra have regularly accepted this defence where it is supported by evidence of normal account usage patterns, no unusual cash withdrawals, and no direct connection to the fraudsters.

What you must not do if this happens: do not withdraw cash, do not transfer any remaining balance, and do not follow instructions from anyone claiming to be "resolving the issue." Those are the fraudsters trying to move money before investigators catch up. Each such action undermines your defence severely.

This is a genuinely winnable situation when handled correctly from the outset.

## Common Cybercrime FIR Scenarios in Pune

**IT professional accused by former employer:** Allegations of data theft, unauthorised access to systems, or breach of confidentiality under the IT Act and BNS. These cases are often driven by corporate disputes rather than genuine criminal conduct. The defence turns on access logs, employment agreements, and whether the accused's actions fell within authorised use.

**OTP fraud where the SIM was compromised:** The accused's phone number was used in a fraud without their knowledge — through SIM swap fraud, phishing, or malware. The victim reports the number, the registered owner is named. Establishing the SIM compromise through network provider records is the foundation of this defence.

**Social media abuse and defamation complaints:** A post, comment, forward, or meme results in a complaint under Section 351 BNS, Section 356 BNS, or IT Act provisions. Context, intent, and whether the content was actually created by the accused or merely forwarded are all relevant.

**Online trading fraud — wrongly named participant:** You invested in a scheme and lost money, but the complaint has been structured so that investors are named alongside operators. Distinguishing investors from perpetrators is the primary legal task in these matters.

**Data theft allegations in corporate disputes:** An employer claims an employee took client lists, source code, or confidential files. These cases frequently arise from legitimate exits gone wrong, and the evidence often falls apart under scrutiny of what was actually taken versus what was authorised to be taken.

## Your Rights During a Cyber Cell Visit

If you do attend the Cyber Cell — whether voluntarily or in response to a notice — these rights apply at all times and cannot be suspended by an officer's instructions to the contrary.

You have the right to have a lawyer present during questioning. You have the right to remain silent on any question the answer to which might incriminate you. You cannot be detained beyond a reasonable period without being formally arrested and produced before a Magistrate. Any statement must be made voluntarily — statements made under duress, inducement, or threats are inadmissible.

If officers ask you to sign any document at the Cyber Cell — including a statement, a consent form for device examination, or any other paper — do not sign without your lawyer reviewing it first.

## What If the FIR Is False or Exaggerated? — Quashing at the Bombay High Court

Not every cybercrime FIR reflects a genuine criminal act. Many arise from commercial disputes disguised as criminal complaints, personal rivalries between former partners or colleagues, exaggerated complaints where the accused's actual conduct was civil in nature, or outright fabrication. If you believe the FIR against you is false or that your conduct does not amount to a criminal offence, quashing is the remedy to seek.

Under Section 528 of the BNSS (formerly Section 482 CrPC), the Bombay High Court has inherent power to quash an FIR where it is satisfied that: the FIR does not disclose a cognizable offence; the allegations, even if taken entirely at face value, do not constitute the offence charged; or the FIR has been filed with an ulterior motive and amounts to an abuse of the court's process.

In cybercrime cases, quashing petitions have succeeded on the following bases in Pune and Maharashtra more broadly: an alleged "hacking" that was in fact authorised access under an employment agreement; a defamation complaint where the content was demonstrably true or constituted fair comment; a fraud allegation where the dispute was purely civil and money was paid under a contract, not through deception; and a harassment complaint where the messages in question were commercial communications that did not meet the threshold of criminal intimidation.

Quashing does not mean the complainant cannot pursue civil remedies — it means the criminal proceedings are terminated. Where the FIR is used primarily as leverage to extract a settlement or apply pressure in a commercial dispute, the High Court views this seriously and courts have repeatedly rebuked complainants who misuse the criminal process.

Quashing is typically sought alongside anticipatory bail in the first phase of a cybercrime defence — if interim protection from arrest is available, the quashing petition can be heard without the urgency of custody hanging over the proceedings. The two applications complement each other and are often filed simultaneously or in short succession.

If you believe your cybercrime FIR is false, motivated, or legally unsustainable, this is the conversation to have at your first consultation with a criminal lawyer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do I have to go to the cyber cell if they call me?**
A: It depends entirely on the nature of the notice. A formal Section 160 BNSS notice creates a legal obligation to appear as a witness, though you may seek exemption or send a legal representative in certain circumstances. An informal phone call creates no obligation. Before going to the Cyber Cell for any reason, speak to a lawyer. Going without advice — even as a witness — can result in your status being converted to accused on the same day.

**Q: Can police arrest me without warning in a cybercrime case?**
A: Yes. Most cybercrime offences under the IT Act and BNS are cognizable, meaning police can arrest without a warrant and without prior warning. This is exactly why anticipatory bail is so valuable in these cases — it prevents arrest before you have had a chance to present your position to a court.

**Q: What if I genuinely do not know why I received the notice?**
A: This is more common than most people realise. The notice may have been sent because your phone number, email address, bank account, or IP address appeared in the investigation without your direct involvement. Do not assume it is a mistake and ignore it. Engage a lawyer who can contact the investigating officer formally and establish the basis for the notice before you decide how to respond.

**Q: How long do cybercrime investigations in Pune take before charges are filed?**
A: Under BNSS, police must file a charge sheet within 60 days of arrest for most offences (extendable to 90 days for serious matters). If you are not under arrest but are under investigation, there is no strict deadline for the charge sheet, and investigations often take six months to two years. This is another reason early legal intervention matters — the pre-charge period is when cases can most effectively be resolved, challenged, or weakened before they reach the trial stage.

**Q: Can an FIR be quashed in a cybercrime case?**
A: Yes — but it depends on the facts. If the FIR does not disclose a cognizable offence, if it is motivated by personal or commercial rivalry, or if the facts alleged — even if taken at face value — do not constitute the offence charged, a quashing petition before the Bombay High Court is available under Section 528 BNSS. This is a powerful remedy and should be assessed early, ideally alongside the anticipatory bail strategy.

**Q: Can I be arrested for something I did not personally post — a forwarded message, a meme, or content someone else sent to me?**
A: Forwarding or sharing content does not automatically make you the author of that content, but it can still attract liability under the BNS or IT Act if the content is found to be defamatory, obscene, or threatening and you shared it knowingly. The key legal questions are whether you knew the nature of the content and whether you intended the communication to reach the complainant. Cases based purely on forwards are generally weaker than cases involving original creation, and this distinction should be made clearly in your defence from the outset.
