# What Happens After an FIR is Filed in Pune? A Step-by-Step Guide

An FIR is filed. A phone call comes. And suddenly a family that has never had anything to do with the police or the courts is trying to understand what happens next — in a system they have never navigated before.

This guide explains the full criminal process in plain language, from the moment an FIR is registered to the final verdict, with realistic timelines for Pune specifically.

---

## What an FIR Actually Is

FIR stands for First Information Report. It is a written document that the police record when they receive information about a cognizable offence — that is, an offence serious enough that police can investigate and make an arrest without waiting for a court order.

An FIR is not a charge. It is not a conviction. It does not mean the person named in it is guilty of anything. It is simply the document that starts the criminal process — the equivalent of opening a file. Many FIRs are investigated and result in no conviction at all. Some result in a final report where police themselves say the allegation was false or unproven. Understanding this from the start matters, because panic in the first hours often leads to decisions that harm the case later.

---

## The First 24 Hours After an FIR is Filed

Once an FIR is registered at a police station in Pune, the station house officer is required to investigate. What happens to the person named in the FIR depends on the nature of the offence.

**For bailable offences** (for example, minor assault, cheating below a certain threshold): the police typically issue a notice asking the accused to appear at the police station. They cannot simply arrest someone for a bailable offence without justification. The accused can offer bail at the police station itself.

**For non-bailable offences** (for example, murder, robbery, serious fraud, drug offences under NDPS): police have the power to arrest without a warrant. If they do arrest, the person must be produced before a Magistrate within 24 hours of arrest. At that point, the Magistrate decides whether to send the accused to police custody (for further questioning) or judicial custody (jail), and bail is argued.

**What should the accused and family do in the first 24 hours?** Contact a criminal defence lawyer immediately — before speaking to the police, before making any statement, and before agreeing to anything. Do not speak to the police without legal advice. Do not give a written statement voluntarily. Preserve all documents, messages, and records that may be relevant to the case.

---

## The Investigation Stage

After the FIR is registered, the investigating officer collects evidence: visits the scene, records witness statements, examines physical evidence, reviews documents, and may call the accused for questioning under Section 61 BNSS (formerly Section 160 CrPC).

If the police call the accused for questioning, the accused must attend — but has the right to have a lawyer present during questioning. The accused cannot be compelled to answer questions that would incriminate them. The right against self-incrimination is constitutionally guaranteed. What you say during questioning can and will be used against you. What you do not say, under legal advice, often cannot.

During investigation, depending on the case, a lawyer may file an anticipatory bail application — this is an application to the Sessions Court or High Court seeking protection from arrest before it happens. Anticipatory bail does not mean the accused is admitting anything. It means the accused is protected from custody while the matter is being investigated, subject to conditions the court sets.

**Realistic Pune timeline for investigation:** Most investigations in Pune take between one month and six months. Complex cases — economic offences, organised crime, cases involving electronic evidence — can take longer.

---

## The Chargesheet (Final Report)

The chargesheet is the document police file in court at the end of their investigation. It sets out the specific allegations, lists the witnesses, attaches the evidence, and formally requests the court to try the accused. This is different from the FIR: the FIR starts the process; the chargesheet formally places a case before the court.

**Deadlines for filing the chargesheet matter enormously.** Under Section 479 BNSS (formerly Section 167(2) CrPC), if the accused is in custody, police must file the chargesheet within 60 days for offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment of 10 years or more. For all other offences, the deadline is 90 days.

If police do not file within these deadlines, the accused in custody has an automatic right to bail — called default bail or statutory bail. This right exists regardless of the seriousness of the offence and regardless of whether the court would otherwise grant bail. It is a right, not a request. A separate detailed post on this website covers default bail under Section 479 BNSS in full.

**Realistic Pune timeline for chargesheet:** Between two and six months from the FIR, depending on the complexity of the case and the investigating agency's workload.

---

## Cognizance by the Magistrate

Once the chargesheet is filed, the Magistrate "takes cognizance" — meaning the court formally takes up the matter and examines whether there is enough on paper to proceed. The court may call the accused for a first hearing.

At the charge-framing stage, the accused has the right to file a **discharge application** — an application arguing that the evidence in the chargesheet is not sufficient to even put the accused on trial. If the Magistrate agrees, the accused is discharged and the matter ends at this stage.

If charges are framed — that is, if the court formally reads out the specific allegations and the accused pleads not guilty — the case moves to trial.

During this phase, a lawyer may also file a petition to quash the FIR under Section 528 BNSS (formerly Section 482 CrPC) before the High Court, asking that the entire case be thrown out on legal grounds: that the FIR discloses no offence, that it is a clear abuse of process, or that the matter has been settled between the parties.

---

## The Trial Stage

Trial is the stage where evidence is tested in open court. The prosecution presents its witnesses, who are examined by the public prosecutor and then cross-examined by the defence lawyer. Cross-examination is where cases are often won or lost — witnesses may contradict their earlier statements, reveal bias, or admit facts that support the defence.

After the prosecution closes its case, the accused has the right to explain any incriminating evidence. The defence may then call its own witnesses.

Both sides then present final arguments, and the judge delivers the verdict.

**Realistic Pune timeline for trial:** This is the longest stage. Simple cases may conclude in one to two years. Medium-complexity cases — with multiple witnesses and documentary evidence — typically take three to six years in Pune's Magistrate courts. Sessions Court cases (involving serious offences) can take five to ten years, sometimes longer.

---

## Verdict and Sentencing

If the accused is acquitted, they are free. An acquittal is not a minor outcome — it means the court found the prosecution failed to prove the case beyond reasonable doubt. After acquittal, the accused can seek to have the FIR record reflected as disposed of in their favour.

If convicted, sentencing follows — either immediately or after a separate hearing. The convicted person has the right to appeal to the Sessions Court (if convicted by a Magistrate), then to the High Court, and then to the Supreme Court of India.

---

## The Most Important Thing to Understand

An FIR does not make conviction inevitable. According to court statistics, a significant proportion of criminal cases in India end in acquittal or discharge. Many are closed during investigation itself when police file a final report saying the allegation is unproven.

The decisions made in the first days — who you speak to, what you say, when you engage a lawyer, whether you apply for bail early — shape the entire trajectory of the case. A lawyer who joins on day one has options that are no longer available by day thirty.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Can an FIR be cancelled or withdrawn?

The complainant cannot simply "take back" an FIR the way they might withdraw a complaint in a civil matter. However, the police can file a final report (sometimes called a "B summary") if their investigation finds the allegation unproven or false. Separately, the accused can approach the High Court to quash the FIR under Section 528 BNSS. In cases involving compoundable offences — where the law allows private settlement — the parties can jointly approach the court or the High Court to have the matter resolved.

### Q: Does an FIR show up on a background check?

An FIR registration itself is a police record. Background verification agencies and police character certificate processes can reveal that an FIR was filed and what its outcome was. If the FIR ends in acquittal or is quashed, that outcome is also recorded. For certain jobs — especially government employment, police verification, or immigration purposes — any FIR history will typically surface. Getting legal advice early protects the record.

### Q: Can you travel abroad after an FIR is filed against you?

In many cases, yes — especially if there is no arrest, no bail condition restricting travel, and no lookout notice (LOC) or lookout circular issued by the police. However, police in serious cases do issue lookout circulars, which can prevent departure from Indian airports. The Passport Act also has provisions that can affect passport validity in certain situations. If international travel is a concern, a lawyer should verify whether any restrictions have been placed on the accused before travel is booked.

### Q: What is the difference between an FIR and a charge sheet?

An FIR is the document that starts the process — it records the initial complaint and triggers investigation. It is filed at the police station. A charge sheet (or final report) is the document police file in court at the end of their investigation, formally placing the case before a judge. Think of the FIR as the complaint and the chargesheet as the police's case once investigation is complete. Between the FIR and the chargesheet, weeks or months of investigation take place.
