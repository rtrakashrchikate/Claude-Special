## When a Business Dispute Becomes a Criminal Case

One of the most common — and most misunderstood — situations facing Hadapsar's business community is when a civil commercial dispute is converted into a criminal complaint. A supplier who hasn't been paid files a cheating FIR. A business partner who feels cheated on a contract alleges criminal breach of trust. A buyer claims the goods were fraudulent.

In most of these cases, what actually happened is a civil matter: a payment default, a contract dispute, or a failed business deal. Indian courts — including the Supreme Court — have consistently held that converting a civil dispute into a criminal complaint is an abuse of process. The critical legal question is: at the time the transaction happened, was there a criminal intention (mens rea), or was it simply a business arrangement that later went wrong?

If you are facing a cheating or breach of trust FIR arising from a business transaction, the right response is immediate and strategic:

- **Do not ignore the FIR or police summons** — failing to appear can lead to a non-bailable warrant.
- **Apply for anticipatory bail immediately** if arrest is possible — this protects you while the case is being contested.
- **File a quashing petition at the Bombay High Court** if the FIR discloses no genuine criminal offence.
- **Gather documentary evidence** — contracts, emails, bank statements, and correspondence that show the commercial nature of the dispute.

Adv. Akash Chikate has handled multiple cases where business partners or creditors filed criminal FIRs over payment defaults. The combination of anticipatory bail, a stay on investigation, and a quashing petition is often the most effective route. Contact us as soon as an FIR is filed or threatened.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: My business partner filed a cheating FIR against me over a commercial dispute. Is this criminal?**
A: Not necessarily — and in many cases, it should not be. Indian law distinguishes between a civil dispute (a breach of contract or payment default) and a criminal offence (where there was dishonest intention from the beginning). Courts have repeatedly held that a creditor cannot use the criminal process simply to recover money that is legitimately owed in a civil dispute. However, an FIR — even a false one — must be taken seriously. You should apply for anticipatory bail immediately to prevent arrest, retain counsel to review the FIR, and consider filing a quashing petition at the Bombay High Court. Ignoring an FIR is the worst option; acting quickly with the right legal strategy can get the case quashed or stayed before serious harm is done.

**Q: I'm a small business owner in Hadapsar. What criminal risks should I know about?**
A: The most common criminal risks for Hadapsar business owners are cheque bounce complaints under NI Act Section 138 (each dishonoured cheque is a separate criminal offence carrying up to two years imprisonment), cheating or criminal breach of trust FIRs from business associates, labour-related complaints if there are disputes with workers, and GST or tax-related criminal proceedings in serious cases. The practical advice is: never issue a cheque unless funds are certain to be available, keep written records of all business agreements, respond promptly to any legal notice or summons, and consult a lawyer at the first sign of a criminal complaint rather than waiting for an arrest.

**Q: How quickly can anticipatory bail be obtained in a business dispute case?**
A: Anticipatory bail (now called pre-arrest bail under BNSS) can be applied for at the Sessions Court or Bombay High Court. In urgent cases where arrest is imminent, we file the same day and seek an interim order. Hearing typically occurs within 1–3 days of filing. Once granted, anticipatory bail protects you from arrest while the underlying case proceeds — it gives you time to respond strategically rather than from custody.
