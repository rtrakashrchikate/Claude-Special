## Protection for Women — Domestic Violence Act

The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 provides a comprehensive civil remedy for women experiencing abuse within a domestic relationship. This applies not only to married women but also to women in live-in relationships, daughters, sisters, and mothers living in a shared household.

Under the DV Act, you can obtain:

- **Protection orders** — directing the abuser to stop violence and harassment
- **Residence orders** — ensuring you cannot be thrown out of your home
- **Monetary relief** — compensation for medical expenses, loss of earnings, and property damage
- **Custody orders** — interim custody of children
- **Maintenance orders** — regular financial support

DV Act proceedings are filed before the Judicial Magistrate. A Magistrate can pass an ex-parte (one-sided, emergency) protection order on the same day of filing if there is prima facie evidence of abuse and immediate danger. The process is designed to be accessible — you do not need to file a criminal complaint to apply for DV relief, though a criminal complaint may be appropriate depending on the severity of abuse.

## 498A / BNS 85 Defence — When the Complaint Is False

Section 498A IPC (now Section 85 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita) makes cruelty by a husband or his relatives toward a wife a criminal offence. In genuine cases, it is an important protection. However, this provision is also misused — complaints are sometimes filed by one party in a matrimonial dispute to gain leverage, driven by third-party pressure, or filed with exaggerated or false allegations that drag in extended family members who had no involvement in any abuse.

If you or a family member faces a 498A / BNS 85 complaint:

- **Anticipatory bail is the first priority** — apply immediately at the Sessions Court to prevent arrest.
- **Review every name in the FIR** — extended family members (parents-in-law, siblings-in-law) are often named without specific allegations and may be separately entitled to quashing.
- **Gather evidence** — medical records, communications, financial records, and witness statements that contradict the allegations.
- **Consider quashing** — where the FIR is vague, lacks specific allegations, or where a genuine settlement has been reached, the Bombay High Court can quash proceedings.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: My husband is abusive. What legal protection do I have under the Domestic Violence Act?**
A: You have several immediate remedies available. Under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, you can apply to the Magistrate for a protection order (stopping the abuse), a residence order (ensuring you stay in the family home), maintenance, and custody of your children. If the abuse is severe — physical assault, threat to life, or sexual violence — a criminal FIR can also be filed simultaneously. One of the most important features of the DV Act is the ex-parte (emergency) order: if you are in immediate danger, the Magistrate can grant a protection order the same day of filing without first hearing the other side. A Protection Officer appointed by the government can also assist in filing your complaint and accessing shelter or medical help. Do not wait to seek help — contact Adv. Akash Chikate for a confidential consultation about your specific situation.

**Q: How quickly can a DV protection order be obtained in Pune?**
A: In genuine emergency cases, an ex-parte interim protection order can be obtained on the day of filing or within one to two days. The Magistrate is empowered to pass such orders without hearing the respondent if there is prima facie evidence of domestic violence and a risk of harm. A detailed affidavit from the applicant setting out specific incidents of abuse, supported by any available evidence (medical records, photographs, screenshots of threatening messages), strengthens the application considerably. After the interim order, notice is served on the respondent and a full hearing is scheduled. Adv. Akash Chikate can prepare and file your DV application quickly — call or WhatsApp to begin.

**Q: My entire family — including my parents — has been named in a 498A FIR. What do we do?**
A: This is a common pattern in matrimonial disputes, and courts have taken note of it. Where elderly parents or siblings are named without any specific allegation of cruelty against them, there are strong grounds to seek quashing of the FIR against those individuals at the Bombay High Court. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against the mechanical arrest of all named persons in 498A cases. The immediate steps are: apply for anticipatory bail for all named persons, and simultaneously assess the grounds for a quashing petition. Adv. Akash Chikate regularly handles these cases and can advise you on the full strategy in an initial consultation.
