## Director and Officer Criminal Liability in Auto Sector

Directors and senior officers in Chinchwad's automobile and auto-component industry face personal criminal liability in ways that surprise many business owners who believe the corporate veil provides full protection. It does not — under several Indian statutes, the individual behind the company can be prosecuted directly.

**Cheque bounce — personal director liability**

Under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, a cheque bounce complaint targets the person who signed and issued the cheque — typically a director or authorised signatory. Even if the cheque was issued on behalf of the company, the individual signatory faces criminal prosecution: up to two years imprisonment and a fine up to twice the cheque amount. Section 141 NI Act extends liability to every person who was "in charge of and responsible for" the company's conduct of business at the time of the offence — meaning multiple directors can be prosecuted simultaneously. Defending these cases requires both challenging the legal basis for officer-level liability and contesting the underlying transaction.

**ESIC and PF defaults**

Failure to deposit employees' ESIC and PF contributions — deducted from worker salaries — is not merely a regulatory violation. Under the Employees' Provident Funds Act and ESIC Act, directors and officers can face personal criminal prosecution, with imprisonment provisions in serious cases. Auto sector companies with large blue-collar workforces must treat PF/ESIC compliance as a criminal risk, not just a statutory obligation.

**GST-related offences**

Fraudulent input tax credit claims, wilful non-payment of GST, or suppression of turnover can give rise to criminal prosecution under CGST Act Section 132. For companies in Chinchwad's auto component sector operating with complex inter-company transactions, the risk is real. Prosecution can target directors and managing persons personally.

**Supply chain fraud FIRs**

When a supplier is not paid — whether due to genuine cash flow problems or a dispute over quality or delivery — the supplier's natural inclination is to file a cheating FIR alleging that the company never intended to pay. These FIRs drag in individual directors by name. The same strategy that applies in commercial disputes elsewhere applies here: anticipatory bail, a stay on investigation, and a quashing petition challenging the criminal character of what is actually a civil payment dispute.

If you are a director or officer of an auto sector company in Chinchwad facing any of these risks, contact Adv. Akash Chikate early — ideally before an FIR is filed or a summons is served.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: I'm a director of an auto components company. Can I be arrested for a cheque bounce issued by the company?**
A: Yes — this is one of the most commonly misunderstood aspects of NI Act liability. Under Section 138 read with Section 141, the director or officer who signed the cheque and any person who was "in charge of and responsible for" the company's business can be prosecuted personally, not just the company. A conviction can carry up to two years imprisonment. The defence strategy typically focuses on two things: first, whether there was proper legal notice served and whether the demand was made correctly; second, whether you were actually in charge of the relevant business decisions at the time. Additionally, if the cheque was for a disputed amount or was given as security rather than for a debt, those are grounds for defence. Retaining a lawyer as soon as you receive a legal notice (which is a mandatory precondition to S.138 complaint) gives you the best opportunity to resolve the matter before it reaches criminal court.

**Q: A supplier filed a criminal complaint for non-payment. What are my options?**
A: Your options depend on the specific allegations in the complaint. If the complaint alleges straightforward non-payment of a bill or invoice, that is a civil matter — a supplier's remedy is a civil suit, not a criminal FIR. However, if the supplier alleges cheating (that you represented you would pay knowing you would not) or criminal breach of trust, you need to respond to the criminal complaint as well. The first step is to assess whether anticipatory bail is necessary — if the complaint has resulted in an FIR and your arrest is possible, anticipatory bail should be applied for immediately. The second step is to evaluate the grounds for quashing the FIR at the Bombay High Court. The third is to assess whether settling the underlying payment dispute would resolve the criminal matter as well — in many commercial cases, a genuine settlement between parties can be the basis for quashing. Adv. Akash Chikate can advise on all three tracks simultaneously.

**Q: There was an industrial accident at our plant. What is the criminal exposure for management?**
A: A serious industrial accident — particularly one involving death or grievous injury — can result in a criminal FIR against the factory owner, managing director, plant manager, or safety officer under BNS provisions relating to causing death by negligence (S.106) or acts endangering life or personal safety (S.125). The Factories Act also has its own criminal provisions for safety violations. The criminal liability question turns on whether management was negligent — whether proper safety protocols were in place, maintained, and enforced. The key advice is: do not make any statement to police without a lawyer present, secure all internal safety records immediately, and retain criminal defence counsel as early as possible. If management personnel face possible arrest, anticipatory bail should be applied for. An early proactive response — demonstrating safety compliance, cooperating constructively with investigators, and addressing worker welfare — also matters significantly in how authorities approach these cases.
