## White-Collar Crime — What "Economic Offence" Means and How Bail Works

"Economic offence" is not a single law — it is a category that covers a wide range of financial crimes investigated by different agencies. Understanding which agency is involved tells you a great deal about what happens next.

The **Economic Offences Wing (EOW)** of the police handles serious financial fraud cases — multi-crore cheating, Ponzi schemes, land scams. The **Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO)** operates under the Ministry of Corporate Affairs and investigates corporate fraud involving companies under the Companies Act 2013. The **Enforcement Directorate (ED)** investigates money laundering under PMLA 2002 and foreign-exchange violations under FEMA 1999. Each of these has different powers of arrest, search, and seizure — and the legal strategy differs accordingly.

Bail in white-collar cases is harder to get than in ordinary criminal cases. For PMLA matters, the law reverses the usual presumption: the accused must show they are not guilty, not the other way around. Twin conditions under Section 45 of PMLA must be satisfied before a court grants bail. This makes anticipatory bail — obtained before arrest — especially important. Acting early, before an agency moves to arrest, is almost always the better position.

Reputation management is a real part of the defence in these matters. We work to prevent unnecessary public disclosure of proceedings while aggressively defending the legal case. If you have received a notice, summons, or learned that your name appears in an agency file, the right time to consult a lawyer is now — not after the arrest.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: I am a company director. Can I be personally prosecuted for offences the company committed?**
A: Yes — and this surprises many directors. Under Section 141 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, every director or officer "in charge of and responsible for the conduct of the business" at the time of a dishonoured cheque can be personally prosecuted alongside the company. Similar provisions exist under GST law, the Companies Act 2013, and PMLA. The key defence is showing that you were not actually in charge of day-to-day operations, or that the offence happened without your knowledge or consent. We have defended directors in both criminal prosecution and regulatory proceedings and know how to build that case from the start.

**Q: The Enforcement Directorate searched my office. What happens next?**
A: An ED search (under PMLA) is serious but not the end of the road. The ED will seize documents, digital devices, and sometimes cash or valuables. After the search, they will typically issue a summons requiring you to appear and answer questions. You have the right to have a lawyer present, and you should exercise it. Statements recorded by the ED are admissible as evidence, so what you say — and how you say it — matters enormously. The next possible steps are attachment of property, arrest, or both. We advise clients immediately after a search to prepare a coherent factual account, identify documents that establish legitimacy of funds, and respond to summons in a way that protects rather than damages their position.

**Q: Can an FIR be cancelled if the dispute is a business disagreement, not a real crime?**
A: Courts have consistently held that criminalising purely civil disputes — breach of contract, business disagreements, property disputes — is an abuse of process. If the FIR on record does not disclose the ingredients of a criminal offence, or if the dispute is essentially a recovery matter dressed as cheating, the Bombay High Court can quash the FIR under Section 528 BNSS. We assess each FIR carefully to identify whether quashing is a realistic option, and if it is, we move the application with supporting documents and precedents.
