## NDPS in Pimpri — The Bail Challenge

NDPS cases in Pimpri fall into two broad categories: personal possession cases (small quantities, often involving workers or individuals on factory premises) and commercial quantity cases (larger quantities, sometimes linked to distribution networks that operate near industrial areas). The distinction between the two matters enormously — both for bail prospects and for the severity of punishment.

Under Section 37 of the NDPS Act, bail in commercial quantity cases is governed by a twin condition: the court must be satisfied that there are reasonable grounds to believe the accused is not guilty, and that he is not likely to commit an offence while on bail. This is a higher threshold than ordinary bail. It does not mean bail is impossible — but it requires a carefully prepared application.

What makes the difference in an NDPS bail application:

- **Quantity and classification** — Was the quantity recovered "small" (personal use), "intermediate," or "commercial"? The thresholds vary by substance and are set out in NDPS rules. A quantity close to a threshold borderline can be challenged.
- **Circumstances of recovery** — Was the recovery procedurally valid? Were panchas present? Was the mandatory independent witness requirement (Section 50 NDPS) followed for personal searches? Procedural violations can be significant.
- **Role of the accused** — A low-level carrier or first-time offender has a stronger bail case than someone in a managerial role within a network.
- **Clean prior record** — First-time offenders with stable residential and family ties are looked upon more favourably for bail.
- **Duration of custody** — If trial is delayed and custody has been prolonged, bail on default grounds may be available.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: I was arrested in an NDPS case in Pimpri. Can I get bail?**
A: It depends on the quantity involved and the specific substance. For small quantity personal possession cases, bail is available before a Magistrate and is obtainable relatively quickly. For commercial quantity cases, the bail standard is significantly higher under Section 37 NDPS — the court must be satisfied that there are reasonable grounds to believe you are not guilty and will not offend again. That said, bail is not impossible in commercial quantity cases. The procedural conduct of the arrest (whether rights were followed, whether the seizure was lawful), your personal background, and the specific evidence against you are all factors that a skilled NDPS bail lawyer will use to build the strongest possible application. Contact us immediately after arrest — the first bail hearing sets the tone for the entire case.

**Q: There was an accident at my factory in Pimpri. Can I face criminal prosecution?**
A: Yes — factory accidents that result in serious injury or death can lead to criminal prosecution of the factory owner, manager, or supervisor under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (sections relating to causing death by negligence or act endangering life) and potentially under the Factories Act. The criminal liability question turns on whether the injury was caused by rash or negligent conduct, and whether proper safety protocols were in place and being followed. If workers or family members file a complaint, or the police register a suo motu FIR following an accident, you should consult a lawyer immediately — before making any statements to police. We advise on anticipatory bail where arrest is possible, assist with compliance documentation that speaks to safety measures taken, and build the criminal defence around the specific facts of the accident.

**Q: A labour contractor filed an FIR against my company for non-payment. Is that a criminal matter?**
A: It depends on the specific allegations. A straightforward non-payment claim is a civil matter — a labour contractor's remedy is to sue for the contracted amount or approach the labour authorities. However, if the contractor alleges that you made false promises with no intention to pay (dishonest inducement to provide services), the complaint could be framed as cheating under BNS Section 318. In such cases, the same analysis applies as in any commercial dispute: was there a genuine criminal intention from the outset, or is this simply a civil payment dispute dressed up as a criminal complaint? We assess the FIR, advise on anticipatory bail if needed, and evaluate the grounds for quashing.
