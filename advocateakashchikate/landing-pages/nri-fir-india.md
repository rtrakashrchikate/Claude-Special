## The Unique Challenges NRIs Face in Criminal Cases

Criminal cases in India become significantly more complicated when the accused is overseas.

**Arrest warrants and Look Out Circulars (LOC).** If police obtain a Look Out Circular against you, your name is flagged at all Indian airports and ports. You could be detained the moment you land in India for any reason — a family wedding, a medical emergency, a property transaction. Getting an LOC removed requires an application before the appropriate court, which takes time.

**Warrants for non-appearance.** If a summons has been issued in a criminal matter and you haven't appeared, the court may issue a non-bailable warrant (NBW). This converts your status from "accused" to "wanted" in the Indian legal system, and makes the situation significantly harder to manage.

**Property attachment.** In financial or matrimonial matters, courts sometimes order attachment of property during proceedings. NRIs with Indian property are particularly vulnerable.

**Inability to attend hearings personally.** Indian courts generally require the accused to be present at hearings. An experienced lawyer can apply for exemption from personal appearance (EPA) in appropriate cases — but this must be handled proactively before a warrant issues.

**Time difference and communication.** Managing a criminal case from the US, UK, UAE, Canada, or Australia across time zones, with paperwork that moves in physical copies, requires a lawyer who is responsive and organised about remote client management.

## Common NRI Criminal Matters Handled

**Matrimonial-origin FIRs (498A / 85 BNS)**
The most frequent category. An estranged spouse or her family files a complaint of cruelty or dowry harassment after the NRI husband returns abroad. The FIR can result in arrest if the NRI returns to India without legal protection in place.

**Property and succession disputes turned criminal**
A property transaction goes wrong. A relative files a cheating or fraud case. What started as a civil matter becomes a criminal case when someone files an FIR under IPC S.420/318 BNS.

**Cheque bounce matters**
Business dealings in India, a payment dispute, a post-dated cheque that bounced — Section 138 NI Act is a criminal matter and notices issued under it require timely legal response from India.

**PMLA / financial crime investigations**
NRI business people and investors in India are sometimes named in Enforcement Directorate (ED) or economic offence investigations. These require immediate representation.

**Cybercrime FIRs**
Online transactions, social media disputes, or business communications can generate cybercrime FIRs under the IT Act. Distance makes these harder to respond to without local counsel.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I get anticipatory bail without coming to India?**
Yes. Anticipatory bail can be applied for in your absence. You provide a sworn affidavit (which can often be sworn before an Indian consulate abroad or notarised). The lawyer argues the application in court. If granted, you will be protected from arrest when you do enter India.

**What if there is already a warrant against me?**
A non-bailable warrant needs to be addressed urgently. We file an application to surrender before the Magistrate (which is controlled and safe when done through counsel) or seek a stay of the warrant from the Bombay High Court, depending on the situation.

**I haven't been to India in years and don't know the current status of my case. What should I do?**
This is common. We run a check on your name in the relevant court's case management system and obtain a current status. We can usually tell you within 24–48 hours what the exact legal position is.

**Can the matter be handled entirely without my coming to India?**
Depends on the matter type. Anticipatory bail hearings, FIR quashing, and most preliminary proceedings can be handled without your physical presence. Trial matters eventually require attendance, but trials take years — and we can apply for EPA during that period.

**I'm based in the UAE / UK / USA / Canada — does that matter?**
No. Time zones are a scheduling issue we manage. The legal process is the same regardless of where you are.
